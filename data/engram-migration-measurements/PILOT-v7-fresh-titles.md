## Substitution tests + insight, verbatim

### 0. [general] Whoever states a principle instead of the exact action guides only those already able to work it out

- "a principle" (generalised from "abstract ethical framework / be kind / respect others"): also covers "use reasonable care" and "act professionally" — claim stays TRUE of both: each guides only a person who can already infer which act is owed, which is why such standards need case law or a mentor to become followable.

_insight in plain speech:_ An abstract rule quietly hands the hardest part — figuring out which act it requires — to the person who has to act. So it only works for someone who already has the judgement it was supposed to supply, and gives nothing to the person who cannot do that figuring; a rule that names the physical act ("don't hit, pinch, squeeze, or bite") has done that work for them.

### 1. [general] Partners spend the relationship on the security meant to protect it, and the security cannot give the relationship back

- "partners" (generalised from "the couple" / "a marriage"): also covers business co-founders and siblings running a family enterprise. TRUE of both — co-founders who grind a decade toward the payout often arrive with the working friendship spent, and the money does not restore it; siblings who finally clear the family debt arrive the same way. Floor check: I rejected "two people" and "a pair", which also cover dance partners and bus passengers — the claim is FALSE there, because nothing of the relationship is being paid out. "Partners" carries the joint enterprise that the relationship is funding, so it is the most general noun that stays true.

_insight in plain speech:_ The couple's years of struggle are the instance; the insight is that a long joint pursuit is funded out of the relationship it is supposedly for, and the prize, when it lands, pays nothing back into that account. The partners are the party who act — they do the spending, and the sources name no outside perpetrator, so I did not invent one; the harm here is self-inflicted by the pursuit. I did not compress to "restores nothing", because arriving does restore the material conditions — what it cannot restore is the relationship, and that distinction is the claim. Two mechanisms in the sources behave differently and belong in separate bullets rather than being fused in the title: three sources say the years wore the bond down, while the fourth says the shared pressure was the only thing holding the pair together, so its lifting exposes an absence rather than a wound. The title states the claim the three share; the fourth source's variant is a bullet, and the disagreement between them gets said outright rather than split.

### 2. [general] Specialists add up to an answer only where a coordinator defines their handoffs and recombines their partial results

- specialists (generalised from 'specialized AI agents'): also covers a surgeon/anaesthetist/radiologist on one case, and a reporter/photographer/copy editor on one story. Claim stays true of both — their separate outputs become one treated patient or one published piece only when a lead sets the handoffs and assembles the parts. Stepped back down from the broader 'workers', which also covers five interchangeable cleaners each doing a separate room: nothing there needs recombining, so the claim would be false.
- coordinator (generalised from the sources' 'task coordination' and 'synthesis features'): also covers a general contractor on a build, and a commissioning editor on an issue. True of both. Not too broad: the claim requires a party doing BOTH jobs, so a coordinator who only schedules and never recombines is an instance of the claim (no answer results), not a counter-example.
- answer (generalised from 'coherent outputs' / 'cohesive conclusions'): also covers a diagnosis and a finished building. True of both — partial findings or framed-and-wired parts only become one diagnosis or one building through recombination. Rejected the broader 'result', which also covers a stack of independent deliverables that need no recombining, where the claim is false.
- handoffs (generalised from 'defined interfaces' and 'share intermediate results'): also covers the file format two systems agree on, and the shift-change notes nurses pass each other. True of both — without them the parts do not join. Kept concrete rather than 'a shared structure', which would cover a shared building or a shared calendar, where the claim is false.
- partial results (generalised from 'intermediate results' / 'analytical outputs' / 'insights'): also covers a lab value plus an X-ray reading, and a draft plus its photographs. True of both — each is a piece, and the single output exists only once they are combined.

_insight in plain speech:_ Splitting a job among specialists does not by itself produce a result. Somebody has to say who hands what to whom, and then put the pieces back into one thing — and that recombining is where the single coherent answer actually comes from. So the specialists own their parts, but the coordinator owns whether the parts add up to anything.

### 3. [general] An institution whose comforts and commerce contradict its professed values lets onlookers judge on sight what specialists otherwise argue

- institution — also covers a charity and a public agency. TRUE of both: a charity's marble headquarters and an agency's plush executive floor are each judged by people who never follow the specialist case against it. Not narrowed to bodies that solicit money, because the mechanism is contradiction-made-visible, not the source of the funds.
- comforts — also covers an executive suite and a members' lounge. TRUE of both: each visibly clashes with a professed frugality and is judged on sight. Cases where luxury matches the profession (a hotel) are excluded by the 'contradict its professed values' clause, not by the noun.
- commerce — also covers a hospital's paid parking and a museum's souvenir shop. TRUE of both: onlookers judge the parking barrier or the marked-up shop without following the financing argument. Chosen over 'luxury', which is FALSE of the sources' leading example — a credit-card tithing kiosk is not luxury, it is a transaction placed where a transaction contradicts what is professed.
- professed values — also covers a stated mission and a public vow of poverty. TRUE of both. The noun is safe only because the sentence states the contradiction; 'professed values' alone would cover professed excellence, which luxury does not contradict, and there the claim would be FALSE.
- onlookers — also covers passers-by and small donors. TRUE of both: each judges the visible thing without following the specialist argument. This is the source's 'observers who might not follow theological reasoning', kept as a distinct position from the specialists rather than collapsed into one audience.
- specialists — also covers a trained theologian and an auditor. TRUE of both: the argument only they can follow is precisely what the visible contradiction routes around. Naming them keeps the two positions distinguished — the specialist argues, the onlooker sees.
- REJECTED as over-broad: 'arrangements' — also covers staffing rotas and supply contracts. FALSE there: nobody judges a contract on sight, so the claim collapses. Stepped down to 'comforts and commerce'.
- REJECTED as over-broad: 'property' — also covers a leasehold and an attribute of a material. Ambiguous rather than general, and reads primarily as real estate, dropping the goods and the payment machinery.

_insight in plain speech:_ When what an institution buys for its own comfort and what it sells on its own floor visibly clash with what it says it stands for, the objection stops being a specialist argument. Any passer-by can look and decide, which is why challengers go after the objects instead of the doctrine — and the institution built the target itself.

### 4. [general] A critic who names the fault in a sometimes-right practice instead of showing it leaves the practitioner disputing a label

- critic — also covers a code reviewer citing an anti-pattern and a manager citing insubordination. TRUE of both: naming the category rather than showing the harm in this case leaves the other side arguing with the name.
- fault — also covers a named anti-pattern (a global variable, a singleton) and a named misconduct category (insubordination, going over someone's head). TRUE of both. It would be FALSE of a context-free fault such as a division by zero, where naming it IS showing it — so 'sometimes-right' is load-bearing and cannot be dropped; it is what steps the noun back down to the level where the claim stays true.
- practice — also covers relying on an expert's say-so and escalating past your manager. TRUE of both: each is right in some situations, so the name alone condemns without settling this instance. (Rejected the broader 'a shared method' and bare 'a defect' — both cover context-free cases that make the claim false.)
- practitioner — also covers the coder whose pattern was named and the employee whose conduct was named. TRUE of both: each is left arguing with a category instead of a demonstrated harm.
- label — also covers a policy-violation citation and a grading-rubric category. TRUE where the labelled thing is sometimes legitimate; a label that carries its own evidence (a measured test result) falls outside the claim, which is why the title says 'instead of showing it' rather than condemning labels as such.

_insight in plain speech:_ When a bad move has a recognised name, the name does the judging. The critic can condemn any instance by reaching for the label, without having to show that this particular instance is bad — and the person criticised is left arguing with the label, since no reason was ever put on the table to argue with. That is the difference the sources draw: a list of named errors produces a verdict with nothing behind it, while asking the questions the label assumes forces the critic to state answers that can be checked and contested. It matters because the named patterns are often legitimate: leaning on an expert, or arguing from someone's character when their credibility is the question, can be perfectly sound.

### 5. [general] A person grieves the future they imagined, or the self their relationship cannot receive, not the other person

- "a person" (the griever): also covers someone whose romance has ended and someone still inside a marriage who has to stay muted — the claim stays TRUE of both, since each grieves either a pictured future or a shut-off part of themselves. Deliberately NOT widened to "a mourner" or "anyone bereaved": someone whose partner has died really has lost the person, which would make "not the other person" FALSE. That is the floor.
- "their relationship": also covers a friendship and a working partnership with a colleague — the claim stays TRUE of both; in each, a person can find no room for their warmth or practical care and can grieve the future they pictured. NOT widened to "a shared arrangement" or "a bond", which also cover a lease or a rota, where nobody grieves a self and the claim goes false.
- "the future they imagined": also covers a shared life pictured years ahead and a hoped-for reconciliation — the claim stays TRUE of both; the sources' "story, hope, or envisioned future" and "projected potential that never materialized" cover each. NOT reduced to "a plan", which is external and drops the never-materialized part the claim rests on.
- "the self their relationship cannot receive": also covers warmth and practical care (the sources' own examples) and any other core trait that works well everywhere else — the claim stays TRUE of each. NOT widened to "something of their own", which would also cover money or time and is ambiguity rather than generality.
- "the other person": also covers an ex-partner and a parent one is estranged from — the claim stays TRUE of both; in each, what is grieved is the griever's own imagined future or muted self rather than that individual.

_insight in plain speech:_ When you grieve a relationship, what hurts is usually something of yours rather than the other person: either the future you had pictured with them, or the part of yourself you had to switch off around them. Those two are different losses, and telling them apart tells you what you actually lost.

### 6. [general] Fighting among people at the same level protects the ones taking a cut of what they produce together

- "people at the same level" — also covers sellers on one marketplace, and tenant farmers on one estate. Claim stays TRUE of both: sellers undercut each other while the platform's commission goes unquestioned; tenant farmers feud over plots while the landowner's portion goes unchallenged. Held at this level rather than "people" or "groups" because the rest of the sentence supplies the condition (something they jointly produce, from which a third party takes a cut), so it cannot be misread onto two rival heads of state, where nothing is being taken and the claim would be empty.
- "the ones taking a cut of what they produce together" — also covers an employer paying less than the value of the work, and a platform charging commission on every sale its sellers make. Claim stays TRUE of both: in each case peer rivalry is what leaves the cut unexamined. Deliberately NOT generalised to "a shared system" or "an arrangement" — that was the measured failure: a grazing commons or a mutual-aid group is a shared arrangement with nobody taking a cut, and the claim is false there.
- "fighting" — also covers undercutting each other on price, and blaming a neighbouring group for lost work. Claim stays TRUE of both: each absorbs the pressure that would otherwise reach whoever takes the cut. Not raised to "disagreement" or "difference", which cover friendly argument between people at the same level, where nothing is protected and the claim goes false.
- "a cut" — also covers wages held below the value of the work, and a portion of a harvest. Claim stays TRUE of both. Not widened to "what they own", which covers ordinary theft: a thief is not protected by peer rivalry, so the claim would fail.

_insight in plain speech:_ When people on the same rung fight each other, whoever is taking a cut of what they all produce is never the one being fought, so the taking carries on undisturbed. The horizontal fight is not a distraction from the arrangement — it is what holds the arrangement up, because it uses up the very grievance that would otherwise be pointed upward. Both sources are explicit that the two directions behave differently: conflict aimed sideways prevents the people involved from recognising, or acting on, the extraction they have in common.

### 7. [craft] A golfer changes how steeply he strikes with ball position, but stance width changes steepness and where his power comes from

- "a golfer" — covers a tour professional and a weekend amateur (the sources speak of both "tour pros" and "the golfer"); the claim stays TRUE of each, because low point under the sternum, centre-of-gravity height, and lead-hip clearance are geometry, not skill. I did NOT step up to "a player" or "an athlete": that covers a tennis player and a baseball hitter, whose ball arrives moving and has no position in the stance, and the claim is FALSE of them. "Golfer" is the floor.
- "how steeply he strikes" (generalised from the sources' "angle of attack") — covers an iron shot, a driver tee shot, and a chip; TRUE of each, since the sources give steep for irons and chips and shallow-to-upward for the driver. Boundary recorded: a putt is also a strike, and ball position still changes its steepness, but nothing in a putt trades rotation against ground force — so the second clause holds only for shots where power is generated, which is what the sources describe.
- "ball position" — covers back of centre, centred, and forward of centre; TRUE across the range, because the low point stays under the sternum, so back intercepts the club descending and forward intercepts it after the low point.
- "stance width" — covers narrow and wide; TRUE at both ends, because narrow gives a steeper strike plus rapid hip rotation while wide gives a shallower strike plus linear ground force. So "changes both" holds whichever way the width moves, and one dial genuinely cannot be turned for steepness alone.
- "where his power comes from" (generalised from "rotational velocity" and "linear ground force generation") — covers rotation around a central axis and force pushed against the ground; TRUE of both, since stance width shifts the balance between them. I stepped DOWN to this from the shorter "his power source", which also covers his strength and his equipment — stance width changes neither, so that noun made the claim false, and it could be read as amount of power rather than origin.

_insight in plain speech:_ A golfer does not need a different swing to hit down or up on the ball — the low point of the arc stays under his sternum, so moving the ball back or forward in the stance is what decides whether the club is still descending at impact. Stance width reaches the same steepness, but it is not the same kind of choice: narrowing raises his centre of gravity, steepens the strike, and unlocks the lead hip to spin fast, while widening lowers his centre, moves the low point back, shallows the strike, and gives him a base to push linearly against the ground. So one lever is free and the other is coupled — he cannot set steepness by width without also settling whether his power comes from turning or from the ground. The previous attempt collapsed the two levers into one conjunction and then said both work "without changing the swing itself", which is false of stance width: width changes what the hips can do, and it named no acting party at all.

### 8. [craft] Whoever prescribes one plot structure hands novelists a default in place of the beats their genre's readers expect

- "plot structure" — also covers the five-act structure and the Story Circle. Claim stays TRUE of each: prescribing five-act to a mystery, or the Story Circle to a romance, displaces the beats those readers expect just as three-act does. Rejected the broader "structure" / "organizing system", which also covers a chapter word-count grid and a scene-list outline — no reader expects a beat pattern from those, so the claim would be false; stepped back down to plot structure.
- "novelists" — also covers a romance novelist and a thriller novelist. Claim stays TRUE of each: both have a reader-expected beat pattern that a prescribed default overrides. Rejected the broader "writers", which covers essayists and reporters, whose readers expect no genre beat pattern at all — claim false there.
- "whoever prescribes" — also covers the author of a craft manual that teaches three-act as the rule, and the designer of a story-planning tool whose template assumes three-act. Claim stays TRUE of each: both hand the novelist a built-in shape. Rejected "anyone who recommends a structure" — a recommendation that leaves the choice open displaces nothing, so the claim is false of it.
- "readers" — also covers mystery readers and romance readers. Claim stays TRUE of each: each set expects that genre's own beats, which is what makes the substituted default a loss.
- "beats" — also covers thriller pacing and a mystery's reveal points. Claim stays TRUE of each; the sources name these patterns explicitly as reader-expectation-bearing.
- "a default" — also covers the hero's journey as the built-in, or a five-act skeleton as the built-in. Claim stays TRUE of each: whichever is built in, it stands in for a judgement the genre and the author should have made.

_insight in plain speech:_ When someone lays down a single plot structure — in a craft manual, a template, or a writing tool — the shape of the story stops being decided by what that genre's readers expect and starts being decided by whatever the prescriber built in. Every one of these systems is only a way of finding where the story turns, so the built-in one is not the correct one; it is just the one the novelist did not choose, and the cost of that lands on the novelist and on readers who came for a pattern they do not get.

### 9. [craft] A trusted chronicler who ties local activists to a distant public's own moral tradition makes rejecting them self-betrayal

- "chronicler" (generalised from "advocacy journalist"): also covers a documentary filmmaker, and a historian writing for a general readership — claim stays TRUE of both, each can tie a local action to a public's honoured tradition and shift the cost of dismissal onto that public. It also covers a private diarist, of whom the claim is FALSE (no public reach), which is why "trusted" and "a distant public" must stay in the sentence — they are what hold the noun at its floor rather than letting it slide to "anyone who tells a story".
- "local activists" (generalised from "protest leaders" / "disciplined self-defense groups" / "grassroots victories"): also covers striking workers, and neighbours blocking a demolition — claim stays TRUE of both. Also covers armed community self-defence groups, which the sources explicitly include, and remains true there. "Protesters" was rejected as too narrow: it excludes the self-defence groups and the policy victories the sources cover.
- "a distant public" (generalised from "a national audience"): also covers a nationwide readership, and readers in another region or country who honour the same tradition — claim stays TRUE of both. It does NOT cover people present at the events, of whom the claim is FALSE — they need nothing made legible to them — so "distant" is load-bearing, not scenery.
- "moral tradition" (generalised from "American prophetic witness tradition"): also covers a religious tradition of bearing witness, and a constitutional founding story — claim stays TRUE of both. Stepping up one more level to bare "tradition" makes it FALSE: a culinary or sporting custom is a tradition, and tying activists to it confers nothing and costs the public nothing. "Moral" is the floor.
- "a distant public's OWN moral tradition" (the possessive, not a noun, but the same test): the tradition must belong to the audience, not to the activists. Tied to the activists' own tradition the claim is FALSE — no leverage exists, because the public owes that tradition nothing. This is precisely the distinction the previous attempt destroyed with ownerless "recognizable".

_insight in plain speech:_ When someone the wider public already credits tells them that a local group's actions carry on a moral tradition that public itself honours, the group stops having to earn standing — it inherits standing the public has already granted. The trap is what that does to the audience: it can no longer wave the group away as disorder without disowning its own tradition, so dismissal costs it something. That is why the telling can weigh as much as what happened on the ground — the account, not the confrontation, is what moved the public's own commitments into play. Two things the sources hold that the title deliberately leaves to the bullets rather than padding the sentence: the tradition must be the audience's and not the activists' (already carried by "own"), and there is a rival narrator racing for the same ground — publishing straight after a victory is what stops the other side narrating first — plus the reframing of a condition as somebody's deliberate choice rather than as fate, which names the party who caused the harm.
