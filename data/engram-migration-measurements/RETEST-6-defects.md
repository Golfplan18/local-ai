# Targeted retest — 6 named defects

Each case had a defect you named, so there is a right answer.

### #3 'commerce' too narrow — church/party enjoy privilege+power

- **prev:** An institution whose comforts and commerce contradict its professed values lets onlookers judge on sight what specialists otherwise argue
- **NEW (19w):** An institution's visible advantages that contradict its professed values turn an expert critique into a target plain to onlookers

_actor:_ The institution's visible advantages — a thing, not a person, and the thing that actually does the converting; forcing a human agent here would obscure it, since no one designs a gift shop as an indictment. The other parties appear once each: the institution that holds the advantages and professes the values, the experts whose critique it was, the onlookers to whom the target is plain.

_conversion:_ IN: a critique that only specialists can evaluate — a doctrinal or technical argument that the institution betrays the values it preaches, unfollowable by the very people whose regard sustains it, and therefore safe. OUT: a physical thing anyone can see and point at — a payment kiosk, a gift counter, plush seating beside bare benches, a privilege on open display — that carries the same accusation without any of the argument. PERVERSE: the institution acquired those advantages for its own enjoyment, and that enjoyment is what makes the case against it legible. Its immunity rested on the argument being too specialised to reach a lay audience; its own comforts hand that audience the conclusion directly. The more it enjoys, the better equipped its challengers, and the thing it bought because it could becomes the exhibit against it.

_substitution, both ways:_
- institution — TOO BROAD: covers a church, a political party, a university, a charity, a corporation, a government agency; the claim holds for each because the sentence requires advantages contradicting values that body itself professes, so a body professing nothing has nothing to contradict and is silently excluded rather than falsified. TOO NARROW: the same mechanism runs on an individual figure who preaches austerity and lives well; 'institution' was kept because the professed values need a named holder and the sources describe organisational facilities, and any wider noun drops that owner and slides toward tautology.
- visible advantages — TOO BROAD: covers privilege, power, wealth, comfort, exemption, commerce, access; the claim holds for each so long as it is on display, which 'visible' states — a hidden tax exemption produces no target, so the bare noun WOULD be too broad and the qualifier is load-bearing, not decoration. TOO NARROW: this is the repair of the previous title's 'commerce', which excluded exactly the privilege and power the owner named; 'advantages' reaches kiosks, merchandise, luxury facilities, reserved comfort, and unearned standing alike.
- expert critique — TOO BROAD: covers doctrinal, ideological, legal, economic, and technical arguments; true of each, because all the mechanism needs is that a lay audience cannot adjudicate it on its own terms. TOO NARROW: 'theological' or 'doctrinal' would lock the claim to religion — the same defect as 'saboteurs' in the owner's standard — while 'expert' reaches every specialist argument the pattern runs on.
- target — TOO BROAD: any thing pointed at; the claim stays true because the sentence supplies what makes this one a target, namely the contradiction with values the institution itself professes. TOO NARROW: it covers both senses the sources give — an object confronted (the kiosk) and a contrast displayed (seating against benches) — so it reaches the whole mechanism rather than only the confrontation half.
- onlookers — TOO BROAD: covers members and outsiders alike, which the sources require, since a congregant who cannot follow the theology still sees the plush seating; the claim asserts only that the target is plain to them, not that each one acts, so it survives the indifferent onlooker. TOO NARROW: 'outsiders' or 'critics' would exclude the institution's own audience, where the mechanism bites hardest; and attributing the confronting to onlookers would over-assert — the sources have challengers confront the symbols while observers merely grasp them, and those two parties behave differently, so the title claims only what the observers do.

### #6 'ones taking a cut' — power relationship unnamed

- **prev:** Fighting among people at the same level protects the ones taking a cut of what they produce together
- **NEW (20w):** Fighting among the people who produce the wealth protects those above them who set the rules and take a cut

_actor:_ Two parties act and both are named once. The people who produce the wealth do the fighting. Those above them set the rules and take a cut — that is the party protected, and naming the position ("above them") together with the two things it does ("set the rules", "take a cut") is what closes the previous defect: a broker or a partner can take a cut, but only a party with power over the producers sets the rules they work under and takes a share of their output. No pronoun stands for an unnamed party: "them" is the producers.

_conversion:_ In: the frustration and competition of the people who produce the wealth — force that exists only because a cut is being taken from them, and the only force that could end the taking. Out: protection for the party above them that sets the terms and takes the cut. Perverse because the grievance the arrangement creates becomes the arrangement's own defence: while the producers contest each other they never collectively examine who benefits from what they make together, and they cannot act together against the party taking from all of them, so the taking continues uninterrupted. The peer fighting is a working part of the arrangement, not a failure of it — which is why the perverse result, not the sideways-redirection mechanism, is in the title.

_substitution, both ways:_
- "Fighting" — too broad: covers rivalry, blame, scapegoating, undercutting, competition for the same slot; the claim holds in each, because in each the contest is spent on someone at the same level. Too narrow: the sources also say attention is directed at each other, not only conflict; "fighting among" carries hostile contest generally rather than only violence, and the milder attention-steering is preserved in a bullet rather than being lost.
- "the people who produce the wealth" — too broad: covers employees, drivers on a platform, franchisees, tenant farmers, subcontractors, sellers in a marketplace; the claim stays true of each, and where no rule-setting cut-taker exists the second clause simply does not engage, so a partnership of equals is not falsely swept in. Too narrow: it must reach anyone whose output is taken, not only wage workers — "produce the wealth" reaches all of them, whereas "workers" (the sources' own word) would have locked the claim to employment.
- "the wealth" — too broad: covers revenue, profit, crops, fees, the proceeds of a sale; the claim holds of each. Too narrow: narrower words from the sources — wages, productivity, labour — would have excluded the franchisee and the tenant farmer, whose cut is taken from proceeds rather than pay; "value" would have been broader but vaguer, and the sources are about economic extraction, so wealth is the most general word that stays true.
- "those above them" — too broad: covers an employer, a landlord, a platform, a franchisor, a governing body, an institution rather than a person, which is what the sources require. The claim holds of each so long as the two qualifiers hold. Too narrow: it must reach institutions and not only individuals, and it does — in ordinary speech "those above them" names the company or the platform as readily as the boss.
- "set the rules" — too broad: covers contract terms, pay formulas, platform policy, tenancy conditions, league rules; the claim holds in each, and each is a case of the producers working under terms they did not write. Too narrow: it must reach any arrangement where the terms are imposed from above rather than only written codes — "rules" carries imposed terms generally, where "contract" or "policy" would have shrunk it to one field.
- "take a cut" — too broad: covers commission, rent, a profit share, fees, tribute; in each the party is taking from what the producers made, so the claim holds. Too narrow: this is the load-bearing extraction, and it must not shrink to money skimmed by a middleman — paired with "set the rules" it reads as a share taken by the party whose terms govern the production, which is exactly the relationship the sources describe.

### #8 actor forced to be a person — 'a plot structure tool' is the actor

- **prev:** Whoever prescribes one plot structure hands novelists a default in place of the beats their genre's readers expect
- **NEW (20w):** A plot-structure tool that prescribes one structure hands the writer its own limits as the beats her genre's readers expect

_actor:_ A plot-structure tool — the thing itself (a framework, a template library, plotting software), which is what actually does the prescribing. Forcing a person into the slot ("whoever prescribes") clouds it, and no qualifier beyond "that prescribes one structure" earns its place: that clause is load-bearing, not decoration, because it is what separates prescribing from offering.

_conversion:_ IN: the single structure the tool was built to express — a limit of the artifact, nothing more. OUT: a set of beats the writer follows as though her genre's readers required them. PERVERSE: the tool is picked up to make a story land with the people who will read it, and it converts its own inability to represent any other pattern into craft authority over that story — so the writer shapes the book to the tool's construction while believing she is shaping it to her readers. Sharpest case in the sources: a tool that advertises a library of structures and then hardcodes its output templates, silently revoking the choice it promised, so the prescription arrives disguised as the writer's own selection.

_substitution, both ways:_
- "plot-structure tool" — TOO BROAD? It also covers a corkboard, index cards, a blank outline template; the claim is false of those, and the qualifying clause "that prescribes one structure" is what excludes them, so the noun is bounded. TOO NARROW? It reaches a framework document, a genre beat-sheet product, plotting software, and a framework's own template layer — every form the sources describe. The only thing it excludes is a human teacher prescribing one method, and forcing a person into the actor slot is precisely the defect being repaired.

### #9 actor over-specified — 'a trusted chronicler' adds nothing

- **prev:** A trusted chronicler who ties local activists to a distant public's own moral tradition makes rejecting them self-betrayal
- **NEW (21w):** A chronicler who ties defiance to a tradition the audience honors turns their loyalty into an indictment of the status quo

_actor:_ A chronicler — the party who supplies the account an audience treats as what happened, and who chooses which tradition the events belong to. It carries no qualifier: not "trusted", not "authoritative", not "advocacy", because the plain role noun already does the work and each adjective narrows the claim without adding to it. The audience is the affected party and is named once; "their" refers to it. "Defiance", "tradition" and "the status quo" are things, not parties, so they take no owner.

_conversion:_ IN: an act of defiance the audience would otherwise dismiss as lawless or marginal, plus the tradition that same audience already venerates. OUT: defending the existing arrangement becomes disloyalty to that tradition — so the audience's reverence for its own inheritance is what convicts the present. The output is perverse because reverence for an inherited line is ordinarily the force that PROTECTS existing arrangements, and here it is the engine that condemns them: the more devoutly the audience honors its own past, the less it can defend its own present. Perverse a second way — the legitimacy the defiant party gains is manufactured out of the audience's conservatism, not out of anything the defiant party did, which is why the sources treat the account as power equal to the confrontation it describes.

_substitution, both ways:_
- chronicler — TOO BROAD? It covers a journalist, a historian, a documentary maker, a movement's own newsletter, a company's official historian; the claim holds for each, because what matters is that the audience takes this account as what happened. TOO NARROW? It must reach past the press, and it does — anyone who tells the audience what the events were. It deliberately stops short of 'advocate': a lawyer citing precedent invokes a revered line but narrates no defiant act, so there is nothing to legitimize and no indictment is produced. Widening to 'advocate' would cover more cases than the mechanism reaches.
- defiance — TOO BROAD? It covers protest, refusal to comply, a strike, armed self-defense, a lone dissident's stand; each needs standing it does not yet have, so the claim holds for each. TOO NARROW? Not restricted to collective or organized action — a single refusal works, so 'a movement' or 'collective action' would be under-generalised. But it cannot widen to 'any new practice': a novel practice tied to a revered lineage gains legitimacy without indicting anything, and the output clause would go false.
- a tradition the audience honors — TOO BROAD? It covers religious, national, professional and family traditions, founding documents, canonized heroes, a movement's own legacy; wherever the audience venerates the line, loyalty to it can be turned. TOO NARROW? Not limited to the sources' 'moral tradition' or 'prophetic witness' — a craft lineage, a constitution, a founder's stated principles all serve. What cannot be dropped is 'the audience honors': a tradition the audience is indifferent to converts nothing, so the possessor of the reverence is load-bearing, not decoration.
- audience — TOO BROAD? Readers, a congregation, voters, a jury, a profession; the claim holds wherever that body is the one holding the tradition. TOO NARROW? Not limited to the sources' national reading public — a single jury or congregation runs the same conversion, so 'the nation' or 'readers' would cover less than the mechanism reaches.
- the status quo — TOO BROAD? It covers current policy, current institutional practice, an existing settlement or arrangement; the claim holds for each, since any of them can be recast as a chosen arrangement rather than an inevitable condition. TOO NARROW? The sources say 'policy conversations', but the mechanism runs on any standing arrangement — a company's habit, a profession's custom, a church's practice — so 'policy' would be the same under-generalisation the owner corrected when he replaced 'his policy' with 'his policy and actions'.
- loyalty — TOO BROAD? It covers reverence, pride, identification with an inheritance; the claim holds for each, because any of them commits the audience to the line's demands. TOO NARROW? Not limited to 'reverence' (the sources' register) — plain membership loyalty or professional pride is enough, so the more general word is the true one here.

### REGRESSION from EXCELLENT — lost 'pays for pieces, not for the work'

- **prev:** A seller who staggers his releases turns a store's brief boost for each new listing into continuous prominence
- **NEW (19w):** A distributor that promotes whatever is newest pays makers for releasing their work in pieces, not for the work

_actor:_ The distributor — a thing, not a person: the platform whose promotion rule favours whatever is newest. That rule is what does the paying, and forcing it into a human owner ("whoever sets the recommendation policy") would cloud it. The qualifier "that promotes whatever is newest" is load-bearing, not decoration: a distributor that ranked by rating, sales rank or editorial judgement would not pay for slicing, and stripping the clause leaves the claim with no mechanism. The affected party, makers, is named once. No unowned pronoun, no passive whose actor never appears.

_conversion:_ IN: a maker's finished work, plus a choice about how many separate releases to cut it into and how far apart to space them. OUT: the distributor's promotion — new-release placement, recommendation weight, series-level linkage — and the sales that follow from it. WHY PERVERSE: the promotion is supposed to help an audience find good work, but it attaches to release events rather than to the work, so the reward tracks slicing and spacing. The same work earns more by arriving in pieces and less by arriving whole; a maker who ships everything at once forfeits the boost by making all of it share one window, and a maker who fragments one work into a standalone, an omnibus and an abridgement is paid three times for the same content. The distributor ends up paying for withholding and repackaging — the opposite of what promoting the newest thing is meant to reward.

_substitution, both ways:_
- distributor — TOO BROAD: covers an app store, a streaming service, a bookstore's front table, a news aggregator, a social feed. The claim holds for each, but only because the qualifier restricts it to distributors that allocate attention by recency; a wholesaler that ships without promoting, or a distributor that ranks by rating, is excluded by the clause rather than by the noun. TOO NARROW: does it reach everything the mechanism reaches? Yes — any intermediary that rations visibility among other people's work. It excludes funders, prize juries and critics, correctly, since they hand out no distribution reach for a release event to renew.

### REGRESSION from GOOD — flipped to the spoken case, relationship noun crept back

- **prev:** A person who states what their leaving would cost turns pressure the other person felt into terms to bargain over
- **NEW (18w):** Someone who leaves the consequence unspoken keeps its power because the other feels it instead of negotiating it

_actor:_ Someone who leaves the consequence unspoken — the party who could impose it and chooses not to say so. Identified, and identified once; the affected party is "the other". Personhood is not over-specification here because the act IS a choice of silence, so the actor must be an agent capable of choosing it (a suitor, an employer, a counterparty, a state keeping a line vague); an impersonal thing cannot withhold. No decorative qualifiers: not "confident", "patient", or "high-value" — a plain role noun carries the claim, and every relationship-specific label ("courtship", "boundary", "partner") is out.

_conversion:_ IN: the other party's own sense of a loss that was never promised to them. OUT: a limit that works as lived pressure, with nothing said that could be answered. PERVERSE: the honest move destroys the thing it announces — naming the consequence converts a felt standard into a term the other can bargain down (or dismiss as a threat), so the party who speaks plainly forfeits the limit, while the party who says nothing gets compliance no one has to own or defend. The insight is what silence PAYS FOR, not how the pressure accumulates.

_substitution, both ways:_
- someone (the party who could impose it) — TOO BROAD? covers a suitor, an employer who never says "or you're out", a landlord, a state leaving a red line vague: in each the other's own sense of the possible loss does the work, so the claim holds. It would fail only where the other cannot perceive the possibility at all — and "the other feels it" carries that condition inside the title, so no qualifier is owed. TOO NARROW? the mechanism also runs on firms and institutions, but they withhold through an agent's silence, so an acting party reaches them; nothing narrower like "a suitor" or "a negotiator" would.
- the consequence — TOO BROAD? covers withdrawal of interest, ending a contract, moving on to someone else, reallocating a scarce opportunity, a penalty: true of each while the other senses it. TOO NARROW? it reaches deadlines, thresholds, patience running out and the possibility of exit — all consequences of the other's conduct. Deliberately NOT "threat" or "ultimatum": those are the SPOKEN form, and using either would flip the claim to the wrong side, which is the defect being corrected.
- its power — TOO BROAD? covers leverage, pressure, credibility, force; the unspoken form retains all of them, so the claim survives each. TOO NARROW? "power" is the sources' own word ("quiet power"); the narrower "leverage" is a bargaining term and would inverse-read as the very negotiation the title says has not happened.
- the other — TOO BROAD? covers a courted person, an employee, a tenant, a counterparty, and a group as easily as an individual; the claim holds of each because the pressure is generated by their own perception. TOO NARROW? it reaches everyone on the receiving end without importing a relationship, a rank, or a sex.
- negotiating — TOO BROAD? covers bargaining, haggling, debating terms, contesting the limit: once stated, all of them become available, so the claim stays true. TOO NARROW? this is the one place the noun under-reaches: the sources also say a stated consequence reads as a THREAT rather than a lived standard, which "negotiating" does not cover. That second effect goes in a bullet; the felt-versus-negotiated contrast is what carries the insight and earns the title's words, and widening further ("answering it") would blur the distinction the sources rest on.
