# Meadows Twelve Leverage Points

## Trigger

Invoked when a mode needs to identify where intervention in a system is likely to matter. The host mode supplies the system, observed behavior, and proposed intervention; the lens supplies Meadows's ordered catalog of leverage points, from shallow parameter changes to deep paradigm changes.

## Core Structure

Meadows ranks leverage points by depth. Lower-numbered points are usually more powerful and harder to change; higher-numbered points are usually easier and weaker.

1. **Power to transcend paradigms.** The capacity to step outside the system's dominant mindset and hold it lightly.
2. **Paradigms.** The shared assumptions from which the system's goals, rules, and structures arise.
3. **Goals.** The purpose the system is organized to achieve.
4. **Self-organization.** The system's capacity to create, change, or evolve its own structure.
5. **Rules.** Incentives, punishments, permissions, and constraints governing behavior.
6. **Information flows.** Who receives what information, when, and in what form.
7. **Reinforcing feedback loops.** Growth or decline loops that amplify change.
8. **Balancing feedback loops.** Stabilizing loops that resist change and return variables toward a setpoint.
9. **Delays.** Time lags between action, feedback, and perceived effect.
10. **Stock-and-flow structure.** The physical or institutional arrangement of accumulations and movement.
11. **Buffers.** The size of stabilizing stocks relative to flows.
12. **Parameters.** Numbers, constants, targets, subsidies, rates, standards, and thresholds.

## Application Steps

1. Define the system boundary and the behavior that needs explanation or improvement.
2. Identify the current or proposed intervention.
3. Classify the intervention by leverage depth: parameter, structure, feedback, information, rule, goal, or paradigm.
4. Ask what deeper leverage point is being left untouched.
5. Check whether the system's goals and rules will absorb or neutralize the intervention.
6. Recommend the shallowest intervention that can plausibly work, while naming deeper points that would change the system more fundamentally.

## Detection Signals

- A proposed fix changes numbers but leaves incentives and goals intact.
- A system repeatedly defeats reforms that look sensible in isolation.
- Analysts disagree about whether to change behavior, rules, information, or purpose.
- A wicked problem needs intervention points rather than a single linear cause.
- A process map shows feedback loops but not where to intervene.

## Critical Questions

- Is the proposed intervention aimed at parameters because they are visible rather than because they are decisive?
- What rule, information flow, goal, or paradigm makes the current behavior rational?
- Which feedback loop will resist the intervention?
- Are delays likely to make the intervention look ineffective before it works?
- Would changing who sees what information alter behavior without heavier control?
- Is the system producing the unwanted behavior because it is pursuing the wrong goal?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Parameter fixation** - Detection: the analysis changes thresholds, budgets, or targets while behavior keeps returning. Correction: inspect rules, information flows, and goals.
- **Paradigm overreach** - Detection: the recommendation jumps to mindset change without an executable path. Correction: pair deep leverage with practical near-term moves.
- **Boundary error** - Detection: the leverage point appears powerful only because the real system boundary was drawn too narrowly. Correction: redraw the boundary around the feedback that matters.
- **Delay impatience** - Detection: a valid intervention is abandoned before the system can respond. Correction: identify expected delay length and leading indicators.
- **Goal blindness** - Detection: reform fights the system while leaving its governing purpose untouched. Correction: state the actual goal the system is behaving as if it has.
