# Social Proof

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that evaluate trends, fads, or rapid adoption patterns — investment, technology selection, market entry — when the justification for action is "everyone is doing it" rather than independent evidence. The host mode supplies the trend and the candidate adopters; the lens supplies the information-cascade analysis that distinguishes wisdom of crowds from herd behavior.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

When uncertain, people look to the behavior of others as a guide for their own actions. This is often rational — the crowd aggregates information. But it breaks down when everyone is copying everyone else and no one has independent knowledge. Bubbles, fads, and stampedes are social proof running on empty signal.

### Mechanism

Under uncertainty, others' behavior is informative when each actor's choice reflects independent information. The aggregate then has high signal density. But when actors copy without independent assessment, the aggregate adds no new information — it only amplifies whatever the original signal was. An information cascade can develop where everyone copies based on copies, with the original signal long forgotten. The behavior persists despite zero underlying evidence.

### Applicability Conditions

- A decision is being made under uncertainty.
- Other actors' behavior is observable.
- The actors' independence (or lack of it) can be assessed.
- The original information source can be traced or estimated.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating all crowd behavior as wisdom or all as herding.
- Ignoring the conditions that distinguish wise crowds from herding crowds (independence, diversity, decentralization, aggregation).
- Conflating social proof with reciprocity or commitment as influence mechanisms.
- Using the lens to justify contrarianism without independent analysis.

### Related Models

- **Wisdom of Crowds** — the conditions under which aggregation produces accurate signal.
- **Information Cascade** — the failure mode when independence breaks.
- **Bandwagon Effect** — synonym in popular usage.

### Worked example

A startup sees three competitors adopt a new pricing model and rushes to match. But investigation reveals all three copied the first mover, who chose the model based on a single blog post with no data. The social proof chain has no independent foundation. The correct response: test the pricing model against your own customer data before following.

## Application Steps

1. Identify the social signal — who are you watching, and what are they doing?
2. Ask: do these actors have independent information, or are they also copying others?
3. Trace the information chain — find the original basis for the behavior.
4. Evaluate that basis on its merits, independent of how many people adopted it.
5. Decide: follow if the underlying reasoning is sound, diverge if it is mere imitation.

## Detection Signals

- A decision is being justified by "everyone is doing it" or "the market has spoken."
- Adoption of a tool, strategy, or investment is accelerating without corresponding evidence of value.
- The analyst is in an unfamiliar domain and feels the pull to copy visible actors.
- A team is converging on consensus too quickly and comfortably.
- Evaluating whether a trend reflects genuine information or mere imitation.

## Critical Questions

- Are the visible actors operating with independent information, or copying each other?
- What is the original source of the behavior, and is it well-grounded?
- Would the analyst adopt this behavior if no one else had?
- Is the speed of adoption consistent with genuine learning or with cascade?
- What evidence would distinguish a wise crowd from herding?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Cascade-as-evidence** — treating widespread adoption as proof of value. Detection: investigation reveals no actor has independent grounds. Correction: trace the chain to its origin and evaluate the origin.
- **Reflexive contrarianism** — diverging from crowds as a strategy regardless of evidence. Detection: divergent decisions are no better than crowd-following on average. Correction: evaluate underlying evidence; sometimes the crowd is right.
- **Domain-asymmetry blindness** — failing to recognize that crowds are wiser in some domains than others. Detection: same analysis applied across domains with very different signal-to-noise. Correction: calibrate expectations by domain.
