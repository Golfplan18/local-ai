# Hanlon's Razor

## Trigger

Invoked from within interpersonal-diagnosis, attribution-audit, conflict-analysis, and charitable-interpretation modes when those modes need a named principle to dampen the human tendency to attribute hostile intent prematurely. The host mode supplies an action whose intent is contested or unclear; the lens supplies the structural-and-incompetence-first heuristic that prevents misattribution from cascading into reciprocal hostility.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by ignorance, incompetence, neglect, or misaligned incentives. Humans systematically overestimate the role of hostile intent in others' actions because the threat-detection system is asymmetrically tuned to false-positive on hostility. Most harmful outcomes flow from mistakes, miscommunication, or bad systems; reserving "malice" as the explanation only when simpler causes have been ruled out prevents conflict spirals that the original action did not warrant.

### Mechanism

Two cognitive defaults combine to produce the bias. First, the fundamental attribution error: we explain others' behavior dispositionally (they are hostile) and our own situationally (I had reasons). Second, threat-asymmetry: missing a real hostile signal is costlier evolutionarily than misreading a benign one as hostile, so the system runs hot. Hanlon's razor is a corrective: explicitly try the non-malicious explanation first and require disconfirmation before escalating.

### Applicability Conditions

- An action by another party has produced a harmful or frustrating outcome.
- The intent behind the action is not directly observable.
- Plausible non-malicious explanations exist (miscommunication, time pressure, different priorities, lack of information, misaligned incentives).
- Treating the action as malicious would have costly consequences (reciprocal escalation, ruptured relationship, reputational damage).

### Common Misapplications

- Applying the razor to actors whose track record provides direct evidence of intent. When the same actor has demonstrated hostile intent repeatedly, charity is no longer the appropriate prior.
- Using the razor to gaslight a victim of actual malice. The razor is a tool against premature attribution, not against well-evidenced harm.
- Treating the razor as a verdict ("it must have been incompetence") rather than a prior to test ("incompetence is the leading hypothesis until evidence falsifies it").

### Related Models

- **Fundamental attribution error** — the underlying bias the razor counters.
- **Mill's principle of charity** — adjacent: in interpretation, prefer the strongest plausible reading of a position.
- **Steelman** — the constructive sibling: not just refuse the worst reading but actively reconstruct the strongest one.

### Worked example

A product manager ships a feature that conflicts with what your team was building. Your gut says they are empire-building and stepping on your work deliberately. Hanlon's razor: they probably did not know your team was working on the same problem, or they interpreted the roadmap differently, or their leadership gave them a conflicting directive. A five-minute conversation reveals they had no idea about your project because the shared roadmap was outdated. The fix is a better communication process, not a political counterattack.

## Application Steps

1. Notice that an attribution is being constructed (someone "did X to me," "is trying to Y").
2. Ask: what non-malicious explanation could produce this same outcome?
3. Enumerate structural causes: miscommunication, different priorities, lack of information, time pressure, misaligned incentives.
4. Test the non-malicious hypothesis directly: ask the person, check for the structural cause, look for prior evidence of intent.
5. Reserve malice as the explanation only when simpler causes have been ruled out and direct evidence of intent is available.

## Detection Signals

- A narrative about someone's hostile intent is forming based on a single action.
- Trust is eroding on a team and members are assigning motives to each other.
- An external party (vendor, partner, competitor) took an action whose intent is being inferred from outcome only.
- The reaction being contemplated is escalatory and would be costly if the attribution is wrong.
- The actor in question has no prior track record of hostility toward the affected party.

## Critical Questions

- Is the attribution being constructed from the outcome alone, or is there independent evidence of the actor's intent?
- Does the actor have a track record that warrants the hostile prior, or does charity remain the appropriate default?
- Has the simplest non-malicious explanation been tested directly, or is it being dismissed without check?
- Would the contemplated response be appropriate if the cause turned out to be incompetence or misalignment? If not, the response is over-tuned to the malicious hypothesis.
- Is the razor being used against a victim of genuine harm to suppress legitimate concern? The razor is a check against premature attribution, not a tool for dismissing well-evidenced patterns.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Razor as verdict** — using the principle to conclude that no malice was present, when the principle only counsels testing simpler explanations first. Detection: the analysis stops at "must have been incompetence" without checking. Correction: treat the razor as a prior to disconfirm, not a conclusion.
- **Charity-as-gaslighting** — applying the razor to dismiss patterns of genuine hostile behavior. Detection: the affected party has documented multiple instances and the razor is being invoked to override the pattern. Correction: when track record provides direct evidence of intent, the razor's prior no longer holds.
- **Single-shot misuse** — applying the razor to a one-off interaction in a context where the cumulative pattern matters. Detection: the analysis treats the action in isolation when its meaning derives from the sequence. Correction: take the sequence as the unit of analysis when patterns are at stake.
