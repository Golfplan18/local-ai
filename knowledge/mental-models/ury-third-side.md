# Ury Third Side

## Trigger

Invoked from within `third-side` (T13) when that mode needs the canonical catalog of ten roles by which a community, third party, or surrounding social system contains, transforms, or resolves a conflict between two parties — distinct from the work the disputants themselves do. The host mode supplies a conflict (interpersonal, organizational, communal, international) and the analyst's read of which conflict-stage drivers are active (escalating tensions, overt conflict, post-resolution containment); the lens supplies the ten-role catalog so the analyst can name precisely which roles are present, which are absent, and which would shift the conflict's trajectory if deployed. The output is a per-role audit (present/absent, by whom), a recommendation set for which roles to develop or recruit, and a flag for any role substitutions where one role is doing work that another role would do better.

## Core Structure

The Third Side is the surrounding community whose third-party action shapes whether and how a conflict between two parties escalates, persists, or resolves. Ury's catalog of ten roles maps the full inventory of constructive third-party functions, sorted by which conflict stage they primarily address: prevention (provider, teacher, bridge-builder address the underlying conditions that produce conflict), resolution (mediator, arbiter, equalizer, healer address active conflict by helping parties resolve it), and containment (witness, referee, peacekeeper address conflict that has not been resolved by limiting its damage). A community with all ten roles operating tends to contain its conflicts; a community missing several roles tends to have conflicts that escalate, recur, or fester.

Each role is presented with: when it is invoked (the conflict-stage signal that calls for the role), mechanism (the work the role does), and example (a recognizable instantiation).

### Prevention Roles

1. **Provider.** *When invoked.* Tensions trace to unmet basic needs (food, shelter, security, recognition, agency, identity). *Mechanism.* Supplies the resource or condition whose absence is producing the tension; addresses the substrate from which conflict grows rather than the conflict itself. *Example.* A community organization that ensures food security in a neighborhood where competition for scarce food drives recurring disputes; an HR function that ensures employees feel recognized, reducing recognition-based grievance escalation.

2. **Teacher.** *When invoked.* Tensions trace to deficits in skills (communication, conflict-handling, perspective-taking) or to absorbed habits (default to violence, default to silence, default to escalation). *Mechanism.* Trains in conflict-handling skills and surfaces the alternative scripts available when the default scripts produce destructive outcomes; this changes the parties' capacities, not the immediate dispute. *Example.* Conflict-resolution training in schools that lowers playground fight rates over years; restorative-justice education in workplaces that changes how grievances are first raised.

3. **Bridge-Builder.** *When invoked.* Parties belong to groups with little contact, mutual familiarity, or shared identity, and tensions trace to in-group/out-group dynamics. *Mechanism.* Creates structured occasions for parties to encounter one another in ways that build relationship, find common ground, and humanize the other; reduces the in-group/out-group distance that fuels escalation. *Example.* Cross-community dialogue programs in divided societies; cross-functional teams in organizations where departmental silos produce recurring inter-departmental conflict; cultural-exchange programs.

### Resolution Roles

4. **Mediator.** *When invoked.* Parties are in active conflict and have positions but underlying interests that overlap or could be reconciled, but cannot reach a settlement on their own because of communication breakdown, emotional escalation, or difficulty surfacing interests. *Mechanism.* Facilitates communication, helps parties move from positions to interests, generates options for mutual gain, and preserves the parties' agency in choosing the settlement. The mediator does not impose; the mediator enables. *Example.* A divorce mediator helping spouses reach a parenting agreement; a Camp David facilitator helping Egypt and Israel surface interests beyond their stated positions.

5. **Arbiter.** *When invoked.* Parties cannot reach a settlement on their own, mediation has failed or is unavailable, but a binding decision is needed and the parties accept the legitimacy of an external decider. *Mechanism.* Hears both sides, makes a decision based on evidence and applicable principles, and the decision binds. The arbiter substitutes external judgment for unreachable mutual agreement. *Example.* A judge ruling on a contract dispute; an HR officer making a final decision on a workplace grievance after mediation has failed; the village elder who issues a binding ruling on a property dispute.

6. **Equalizer.** *When invoked.* Conflict involves a power asymmetry severe enough that the weaker party cannot meaningfully participate in resolution; the stronger party can simply impose its preferred outcome. *Mechanism.* Strengthens the weaker party — supplies allies, information, organizing capacity, or external leverage — until a meaningful negotiation becomes possible. The equalizer's work is to create the conditions for resolution, not to impose a particular outcome. *Example.* A union supplying collective leverage to workers facing an employer; a legal-aid organization providing representation to tenants in landlord-tenant disputes; international advocacy that creates costs for a state's repression of a domestic minority.

7. **Healer.** *When invoked.* Conflict has produced injury (physical, emotional, relational) and the injury itself is now an obstacle to resolution and a generator of further conflict. *Mechanism.* Addresses the injury directly — through acknowledgment, restitution, ritual, or therapeutic work — so that the wound stops driving the parties' behavior. Healing is distinct from settlement: a settlement can be reached without healing (and then quickly unraveled), and healing can occur without a formal settlement (allowing parties to coexist without resolving every issue). *Example.* Truth-and-reconciliation processes after mass violence; restorative-justice circles where offender, victim, and community work through the harm; therapist-mediated couple work where the relational injury blocks practical agreement.

### Containment Roles

8. **Witness.** *When invoked.* Conflict is occurring or escalating and there is no public account of what is happening; the absence of witness allows escalation by removing accountability. *Mechanism.* Makes the conflict visible — to those involved, to the surrounding community, and to relevant external authorities. The witness does not intervene; the witness records, reports, and refuses to look away. The visibility itself often constrains escalation. *Example.* Human-rights monitors in conflict zones; ombudspersons in organizations; independent journalism in contexts where state or corporate misconduct relies on opacity.

9. **Referee.** *When invoked.* Parties are in conflict and the conflict is occurring within a domain that has rules (formal or norm-based), and rule violations are escalating the conflict or producing harm beyond the original dispute. *Mechanism.* Enforces the rules — calls fouls, imposes penalties, restores the conditions under which the dispute can be conducted constructively. The referee does not resolve the underlying dispute; the referee constrains how the dispute is conducted. *Example.* A sports referee in a heated game; a parliamentary chair maintaining order in a debate; a workplace policy enforcer maintaining anti-harassment rules during a contentious project disagreement.

10. **Peacekeeper.** *When invoked.* Conflict has produced or threatens violence, and the parties are unable or unwilling to refrain from it; immediate physical separation is needed before any other resolution work can occur. *Mechanism.* Physically interposes — through presence, through enforcement capacity, or through structural separation — to prevent further violence. The peacekeeper does not resolve; the peacekeeper buys time and protects life so that other roles can do their work. *Example.* UN peacekeeping forces between hostile states; bouncers in bars; school monitors who physically separate fighting students; family members who interpose during domestic escalations.

### Cross-Role Notes

A community's third-side capacity is the inventory of these ten roles and how reliably each can be activated. Conflicts that look intractable often turn out to suffer from missing roles rather than from intractability per se: a long-running grievance with no healer remains hot; a power-asymmetric conflict with no equalizer never reaches negotiation; an opaque conflict with no witness escalates because there is no accountability. The Third Side audit is the systematic check for which roles are present and active, which are present but inactive, and which are absent.

Two role substitutions are common failure patterns. First, an arbiter being deployed where mediation would suffice substitutes external judgment for the parties' own work and weakens their future conflict-handling capacity. Second, a peacekeeper deployed long-term without the resolution roles (mediator, healer) becoming active produces stable but unresolved conflict — the violence is contained but the underlying conflict persists indefinitely (the chronic-peacekeeping pattern visible in some long-running international deployments).

## Application Steps

1. Receive the conflict description and stage signals from the host mode.
2. Audit each of the ten roles for the conflict's surrounding community: present and active, present but inactive, or absent.
3. Identify which roles are most needed for the conflict's current stage (prevention, resolution, containment); flag missing roles whose absence is plausibly contributing to escalation, intractability, or chronic unresolution.
4. Recommend roles to develop or recruit; for each, name the function the role would serve in this specific conflict.
5. Audit role substitutions: is any role doing work that another role would do better (e.g., arbiter where mediator would suffice; peacekeeper where healer is needed)?
6. Return the per-role audit, the recruitment recommendations, and the substitution flags to the host mode.

## Detection Signals

- A conflict has persisted, recurred, or escalated despite the disputants' efforts; the surrounding community's contribution may be the missing variable.
- The host mode `third-side` is dispatching and the dispatch invokes this lens explicitly.
- A conflict-resolution intervention is being designed and the analyst needs to specify what kind of third-party support is required.
- A conflict is being framed as bilateral when in fact a community context is shaping its dynamics; the framing needs widening.
- An analyst is in danger of recommending a single intervention (typically mediation) when the conflict requires several different roles operating in concert.
- A peacekeeping deployment has stabilized violence but resolution has not progressed; the role-mix needs reassessment.

## Critical Questions

- Has each role been audited for actual presence and activity, or has the analyst assumed roles are operating because formal positions exist?
- When a role is recommended for recruitment, is there a candidate (person, institution, structure) actually available to occupy it, or is the recommendation aspirational?
- Has the analyst distinguished prevention, resolution, and containment roles, and matched recommendations to the conflict's current stage?
- When mediation is recommended, are the conditions for mediation (parties' interests overlap; communication breakdown is the obstacle; parties accept the mediator's legitimacy) actually met, or is mediation being applied to a conflict that needs a different role?
- Has the equalizer role been considered when power asymmetry is severe, or has the analyst defaulted to mediation despite the asymmetry making genuine mediation impossible?
- When peacekeeping is in place, is there an active path to resolution, or is the conflict being chronically contained without progress?
- Is the analyst projecting the framework onto a conflict whose surrounding community is genuinely too thin or fragmented to supply the roles? In some contexts the third side simply isn't there to be activated, and the recommendation must be developmental (build the third side) rather than prescriptive.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Mediation-as-default** — recommending mediation regardless of the conflict's structure. Detection: the analyst recommends mediation without checking whether the conflict's obstacle is communication breakdown (where mediation helps) vs. power asymmetry (where equalizer is needed) vs. injury (where healer is needed). Correction: diagnose the obstacle first; match the role to the obstacle.
- **Arbiter-substitution-for-mediator** — deploying an arbiter where mediation would have produced a more durable settlement, because arbitration is faster or the third party prefers to decide. Detection: a binding decision was imposed on parties whose interests could have been reconciled through facilitated negotiation. Correction: try mediation first when the conditions for it exist; reserve arbitration for cases where mediation has failed or is unavailable.
- **Chronic-peacekeeping** — peacekeeping in place for years without the resolution roles becoming active. Detection: violence is contained but no progress toward settlement; the peacekeeping commitment expands rather than tapers. Correction: pair peacekeeping with active mediation, healing, and bridge-building work; treat indefinite peacekeeping as a failure mode, not a stable equilibrium.
- **Witness-without-action** — visible documentation of escalating conflict produces no behavioral change in the parties. Detection: monitoring reports accumulate but parties act as if unobserved. Correction: the witness role requires that the visibility actually create accountability costs; supplement with referee, equalizer, or institutional pressure that acts on the visibility.
- **Healer-omission-after-settlement** — formal settlement is reached without addressing the relational or communal injury the conflict produced; the settlement quickly unravels. Detection: a settlement is in place but parties cannot work together, return to dispute, or transmit the original conflict to the next generation. Correction: distinguish settlement from healing; pair the settlement with truth-telling, acknowledgment, restitution, or ritual work.
- **Provider-blindness** — addressing the surface conflict without addressing the resource scarcity, recognition deficit, or basic-needs gap that is generating the conflict pattern. Detection: conflicts of the same form recur with different specific parties. Correction: ask whether the conflict pattern would persist if the underlying needs were met; if not, the provider role is the higher-leverage intervention.
- **Equalizer-omission-in-asymmetry** — applying mediation or arbitration to a severely asymmetric conflict in which the weaker party cannot meaningfully participate or refuse. Detection: the "settlement" mirrors what the stronger party would have imposed unilaterally. Correction: deploy equalizer roles first to create the conditions under which mediation or arbitration is genuinely possible.

## Source Citations

- Ury, William (2000). *The Third Side: Why We Fight and How We Can Stop*. Penguin Books. The canonical exposition of the ten roles and the prevention/resolution/containment categorization. Revised edition of *Getting to Peace* (1999).
- Ury, William (1999). *Getting to Peace: Transforming Conflict at Home, at Work, and in the World*. Viking. First edition, with extended treatment of the role-catalog's anthropological grounding (Bushman conflict-handling, Semai, Mbuti, and other low-violence-society field studies).
- Fisher, Roger, William Ury, and Bruce Patton (1991). *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In* (2nd edition). Penguin. Foundational negotiation framework that complements the third-side mediation role; the Fisher-Ury principles of separating people from problem, focusing on interests, and inventing options for mutual gain feed into the mediator's working method.
- Lederach, John Paul (2003). *The Little Book of Conflict Transformation*. Good Books. Companion treatment from the conflict-transformation tradition; emphasizes long-term relational and structural transformation, complementary to Ury's role-catalog framing.
- Folger, Joseph P., and Robert A. Baruch Bush (2014). *The Promise of Mediation: The Transformative Approach to Conflict* (2nd edition). Jossey-Bass. Detailed treatment of the mediator role consistent with Ury's facilitative-not-directive emphasis.
- Related: Fisher-Ury principled-negotiation framework (the disputants' counterpart to the third-side framework); Lax-Sebenius integrative-distributive negotiation taxonomy (provides additional structure for the mediator role's option-generation work).
