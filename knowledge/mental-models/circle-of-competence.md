# Circle of Competence

*A lens that distinguishes the domain in which an actor has earned, tested knowledge from the domain in which they do not, and treats knowing the boundary as more important than expanding the circle's size.*

---

## Trigger

Invoked when an actor is making a high-stakes decision in a domain they rarely operate in, when overconfidence might be present, or when choosing between acting and delegating. The lens supplies the boundary discipline: the size of one's competence is less important than knowing precisely where it ends, because mistakes outside the boundary are the most expensive.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Everyone has areas where they have genuine, earned knowledge and areas where they do not. The edge of competence is where mistakes become expensive. Knowing the boundary matters more than the size of the circle. Buffett: "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."

### Mechanism

Genuine competence is built through repeated decisions in a domain with feedback that reveals errors and refines pattern recognition. Outside the circle, an actor lacks the patterns that distinguish a realistic projection from an optimistic one, a robust signal from a noisy one, or an ordinary risk from a tail risk. The catastrophic cost of acting outside the circle is not "I might be wrong" — it is "I lack the patterns to even know how I might be wrong." Knowing the boundary lets the actor partner, defer, or pass; not knowing the boundary leaves them confidently making decisions whose error modes are invisible to them.

### Applicability Conditions

- The decision has consequences material enough that getting it wrong is costly.
- The actor can introspect on their actual track record in the domain.
- An expert or partner exists who could be consulted or substituted.
- The actor can choose between acting, partnering, deferring, or passing.

### Common Misapplications

- Confusing intelligence or reading-knowledge with competence; the circle is built from decisions and their consequences, not from study.
- Treating the circle as fixed; competence can be expanded but only by accumulating decisions in the new domain over time.
- Using the circle as a license to refuse all unfamiliar decisions; some decisions cannot be deferred and must be made on imperfect competence.
- Overestimating the circle in adjacent domains; competence in software engineering does not transfer wholesale to enterprise sales.

### Related Models

- **Dunning-Kruger Effect** — the bias that makes the boundary invisible to those most outside it.
- **Inside View vs. Outside View** (Kahneman/Tversky) — the related discipline of recognizing when one's domain knowledge is insufficient.
- **Epistemic Humility** — the broader posture that the circle-of-competence operationalizes.

## Application Steps

1. Define the domain of the decision precisely — not "business" but "negotiating commercial real estate leases in this market."
2. Honestly assess depth: have you made real decisions here before, seen consequences, learned from failures?
3. If inside the circle, act with conviction; you have earned the right.
4. If on the boundary, proceed with extra caution and seek disconfirming evidence.
5. If outside, either learn before acting, partner with someone inside their circle, or pass entirely.

## Detection Signals

- A high-stakes decision is being made in a domain where the actor lacks a track record.
- Confidence is high but the actor cannot cite specific past decisions in this exact domain.
- Adjacent-domain competence is being treated as transferring wholesale.
- A decision is being made under time pressure that prevents the actor from acquiring the missing competence.
- An expert is available but not being consulted because "I can figure this out."

## Critical Questions

- Has the domain been defined precisely enough that the competence claim can be tested? "Business" is too broad; specific deal types, markets, and counterparty profiles are testable.
- Does the actor have a track record in this exact domain (decisions made, consequences observed, errors corrected), or only in adjacent domains?
- Is the decision urgent enough that competence cannot be acquired or substituted, or is there time to partner or defer?
- What specific failure modes are invisible to the actor outside their circle? If none can be named, the actor may be operating with Dunning-Kruger blindness.
- Is "I'll figure it out" being used to avoid the discomfort of partnering or deferring, when the stakes warrant either?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Reading-as-competence** — study is mistaken for earned knowledge. Detection: the actor cites concepts but cannot describe specific decisions made and their outcomes. Correction: separate study from competence; competence requires decisions with consequences.
- **Adjacent-domain transfer** — competence in one domain is assumed to carry into a related but distinct one. Detection: the actor's track record is in domain X but the decision is in domain Y; the patterns may not transfer. Correction: identify the specific patterns that do and do not transfer; rely only on the transferring patterns.
- **Boundary blindness** — the actor cannot see the edge of their circle. Detection: confidence is uniformly high regardless of actual competence variation. Correction: third-party assessment of the actor's track record by domain; the assessor often sees the boundary that the actor cannot.
- **Defer-everything paralysis** — the lens is used to refuse all decisions outside the narrowest competence. Detection: the actor refuses decisions that must be made and that no available expert can make for them. Correction: distinguish "outside the circle, defer" from "outside the circle but must decide; proceed with maximum humility and explicit error-mode tracking."
