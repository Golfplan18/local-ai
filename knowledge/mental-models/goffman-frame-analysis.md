# Goffman Frame Analysis

## Trigger

Invoked from within `frame-audit` (T1) when that mode needs the foundational sociological account of how frames organize subjective involvement in events. The host mode supplies an artifact, situation, or interaction whose meaning depends on participants sharing (or contesting) a frame; the lens supplies Goffman's machinery — primary frameworks, keyings, fabrications, and frame breaks — for diagnosing what frame is in play, how it was established, and what would shift if it were re-keyed or broken.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

A frame is a *principle of organization* governing the subjective involvement of participants in an event. The same physical sequence ("a man strikes another man") can be understood as a fight, a play rehearsal, a self-defense demonstration, a film scene, a ritual reenactment, or an act of abuse — each of these is a different *frame*, and the frame, not the physical sequence, determines what the event *is* for the participants. Frames are tacit answers to the question "what is going on here?" and are typically taken for granted until something breaks them.

### Mechanism

Goffman distinguishes two kinds of *primary frameworks* — frames that organize experience without reference to any prior framing. **Natural frameworks** treat events as undirected, unguided, occurrences of the natural world (a leaf falling, a body aging). **Social frameworks** treat events as guided doings — actions with an agent who is responsible, motivated, and accountable (a leaf placed, a person aging gracefully). The choice between these frameworks for any given event is consequential: treating an event as natural absolves agents of responsibility; treating it as social makes responsibility intelligible.

Once a primary framework is in place, it can be *transformed* through what Goffman calls **keyings**. A keying is "a set of conventions by which a given activity, one already meaningful in terms of some primary framework, is transformed into something patterned on this activity but seen by the participants to be something quite else." Goffman names five keyings: *make-believe* (play, fantasy, theatrical performance), *contests* (sport as ritualized fighting), *ceremonials* (weddings, ceremonies that re-key acts as their official versions), *technical redoings* (rehearsals, demonstrations, drills), and *regroundings* (when an act is redone for ulterior motives — fundraising galas re-keying socializing as charitable work). The primary framework is preserved as the substrate; the keying determines how participants are involved.

Distinct from keyings are **fabrications** — intentional efforts by one or more participants to manage the activity so that another participant is induced to have a *false* belief about what is going on. Fabrications come in two forms: *benign* (surprise parties, harmless practical jokes, beneficial deceptions) and *exploitative* (cons, frauds, manipulations). Whereas a keying is shared by all participants (everyone knows the play is a play), a fabrication is asymmetric — the contained party doesn't know the frame the containing parties are operating in.

**Frame breaks** occur when the established frame collapses: an actor breaks character mid-performance, a confidence game is exposed, the participants discover that what they took to be reality is staged (or what they took to be staged is real). Frame breaks are diagnostically valuable — they reveal the frame retrospectively by showing what was being held in place and at what cost.

### Applicability Conditions

- The artifact or situation is one where participants' understanding of "what is going on" is consequential (legal proceedings, political events, media coverage, ritual occasions, professional encounters).
- Multiple frames are plausible candidates for organizing the same physical sequence, and the choice among them matters.
- The frame is contested, ambiguous, or disputed by parties with different stakes in how the event is understood.
- The analyst is examining how frames are *established*, *maintained*, *transformed*, or *broken* in the situation under analysis — not merely classifying the event.
- The artifact contains evidence of keyings (theatrical re-takes, ceremonial markings, drill notations) or fabrications (deceptions, set-ups, undisclosed staging).

### Common Misapplications

- **Frame-as-perspective collapse.** Treating "frame" as a synonym for "perspective" or "point of view." For Goffman, a frame is a structural property of the event itself as understood by participants, not a private cognitive stance. Two parties can share a frame while having different perspectives within it.
- **Single-frame foreclosure.** Identifying *the* frame and treating other candidate frames as errors. Goffman's apparatus is designed to surface the *competition* among frames, including frames being suppressed by the dominant framing.
- **Keying-fabrication conflation.** Treating a fabrication as a keying. The asymmetry is the diagnostic difference — if any participant is being deceived about what is going on, it is a fabrication, not a keying.
- **Reductive natural-framing.** Treating a social event as a natural one to absolve agents (the "it just happened" framing in cases of structural harm). Surfacing this move is exactly what frame-audit exists to do.

### Related Models

- **Entman framing functions** — operational complement; where Goffman provides the structural account of frames, Entman provides the four functions (define problem, diagnose cause, make moral judgment, suggest remedy) frames perform in communication.
- **Lakoff frames** — overlapping but distinct; Lakoff's frames are cognitive-semantic structures activated by words, often examined politically. Goffman's frames are situational organizations of experience that may be wholly nonverbal.
- **Stanley propaganda** — examines how undemocratic frames are inserted as if they were democratic; uses framing as a mechanism of ideological capture.
- **Bateson play and metacommunication** — antecedent; Goffman acknowledges Bateson's "this is play" as the prototype keying.
- **Tannen and conversational frames** — extends Goffman into the analysis of everyday talk and the negotiation of interactional alignments.

## Application Steps

1. Receive the artifact, situation, or interaction from the host mode.
2. Identify the **primary framework** in play: is the event being treated as natural (no agent responsible) or social (an agent is responsible, accountable, motivated)?
3. Detect any **keyings** at work: is the event a make-believe (play), contest, ceremonial, technical redoing, or regrounding of some primary activity?
4. Detect any **fabrications**: are some participants being induced to operate in a frame that other participants know to be false? Identify the containing and contained parties.
5. Identify any **frame breaks** (occurred or impending): moments where the frame is exposed, contested, or collapsing.
6. Surface the **alternative frames** that could organize the same event differently; note what is at stake in the choice.
7. Return the framing analysis to the host mode: primary framework + keyings + fabrications + frame-breaks + alternative frames + stakes.

## Detection Signals

- The artifact's meaning depends on participants accepting (or contesting) "what is going on" — i.e., the frame is itself a topic of dispute.
- The same physical sequence is being characterized differently by different parties in ways that matter.
- The artifact contains markers of theatrical, ceremonial, or technical reframing (rehearsal notations, ceremonial language, drill instructions).
- There are signs of asymmetric understanding — some participants know more about what is going on than others (potential fabrication).
- Something has happened to break the established frame (an actor stepping out of role, an exposure of staging, a contest of frames going public).
- The host mode flags that the artifact's organization-of-experience needs to be made explicit before any substantive analysis can proceed.

## Critical Questions

- Has the *primary framework* been correctly identified, or is the analyst assuming a social framing where the participants understood the event as natural (or vice versa)?
- Are the *keyings* shared by all participants, or is the analyst conflating a keying (shared) with a fabrication (asymmetric)?
- Has the analyst identified the *containing* and *contained* parties of any fabrications, including the analyst's own potential containment?
- Are *alternative frames* surfaced, or has the analyst settled on a single dominant framing without examining what else is in competition?
- When a *frame break* has occurred, has the analyst used it diagnostically — to reveal what frame was being held — rather than treating it as merely a disruption?
- Is the analysis open to its own framing being itself a frame, with its own primary framework and possible keyings, rather than a neutral view from nowhere?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Frame-as-bias substitution** — the analyst treats "frame" as equivalent to "bias" and proceeds to evaluate frames by their alignment with the analyst's preferred framing, rather than analyzing how frames structure participant experience. Detection: the analysis converges on "the parties are biased and should adopt the correct frame." Correction: defer evaluation; first do the descriptive work of identifying primary framework, keyings, and stakes.
- **Keying-fabrication conflation** — a play, contest, or ceremonial is mis-described as a deception, or vice versa. Detection: the analysis treats a shared theatrical convention as a manipulation, or treats a manipulation as if all parties were in on the joke. Correction: apply the asymmetry test — is any party being induced to hold a false belief about what is going on?
- **Single-frame foreclosure** — the analyst identifies one frame as *the* frame and proceeds without acknowledging the alternative frames in competition. Detection: the analysis would not survive an examination of why other parties might frame the event differently. Correction: enumerate the candidate frames before settling on a dominant analysis; treat the dominance itself as analytically interesting.
- **Frame-break trivialization** — the analyst notices a frame break but treats it as a curiosity rather than as the diagnostic moment it is. Detection: the frame break is reported but not used to reveal what was being held in place. Correction: use the frame break as a probe — what frame was sustaining itself, at what cost, and what reveals when it breaks?
- **Analyst-frame invisibility** — the analyst's own framing is treated as transparent rather than as itself a frame. Detection: the analysis describes "what is really going on" without acknowledging the perspectival commitment of that description. Correction: include the analyst's framing in the inventory of frames in play; identify what would shift under an alternative analytical frame.

## Source Citations

- Goffman, Erving (1974). *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. The originating text. Develops primary frameworks, keyings, fabrications, and frame breaks in 600+ pages of detailed exemplification.
- Goffman, Erving (1959). *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*. Doubleday. Earlier work that develops the dramaturgical vocabulary (front/back stage, performance, team) on which frame analysis builds.
- Bateson, Gregory (1955). "A Theory of Play and Fantasy." In *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972). Antecedent; the "this is play" formulation that becomes Goffman's keying.
- Tannen, Deborah (1993). *Framing in Discourse*. Oxford University Press. Extends Goffman into conversational analysis; useful when frame-audit is applied to talk.
- Snow, David A. & Benford, Robert D. (1988). "Ideology, Frame Resonance, and Participant Mobilization." *International Social Movement Research* 1:197-217. Operationalizes Goffman for social-movement analysis; develops "frame alignment" and "frame resonance" extensions.
