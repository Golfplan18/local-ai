# Allison's Three Lenses

*A lens that decomposes any major organizational decision into three competing explanatory models — Rational Actor, Organizational Process, and Bureaucratic Politics — each of which surfaces different causes invisible to the others.*

---

## Trigger

Invoked when the analyst confronts an organizational or governmental decision that appears irrational, contradictory, or inconsistent with the organization's stated interests, and a single rational-actor explanation produces an unsatisfying account. The lens supplies three parallel explanatory models so the analyst can run each independently and compare the resulting accounts.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Any major organizational decision can be analyzed through three distinct models, each revealing different causes. **Model I (Rational Actor)** treats the organization as a unitary agent maximizing strategic goals. **Model II (Organizational Process)** explains behavior as the output of standard operating procedures, existing routines, and the limited repertoire of pre-built actions the organization can perform. **Model III (Bureaucratic Politics)** sees decisions as the result of bargaining, coalition-building, and turf wars among individuals with different interests. The most complete account usually combines elements of all three.

### Mechanism

Organizations are simultaneously goal-pursuing entities, rule-bound machines, and coalitions of interest-bearing actors. Model I assumes the goal-pursuing layer dominates; this is true for some decisions but false for many. Model II captures the inertia and path-dependence of established procedures, which constrain even rational leaders to choose from the menu the organization can actually execute. Model III captures the political economy inside the organization: who proposes what, who allies with whom, whose preferences win the internal contest. The three lenses are not exclusive; they are *parallel* projections of the same decision onto different causal planes.

### Applicability Conditions

- The decision involves a complex organization with multiple sub-units and established routines, not a single individual.
- The decision is consequential enough that internal politics and procedures could plausibly shape it.
- The Model-I rational-actor account produces an unsatisfying explanation (the organization appears to act against its own stated interests, or the decision is internally contradictory).
- Sufficient information exists about the organization's procedures and internal politics to populate Models II and III.

### Common Misapplications

- Applying to individual decisions where there is no organizational layer; Models II and III collapse to Model I.
- Treating the three models as competing hypotheses where only one is true; they are parallel projections, and the most accurate account combines all three.
- Defaulting to Model III (bureaucratic politics) as a cynical catch-all when Models I and II would supply a cleaner explanation.

### Related Models

- **Bounded Rationality** — the cognitive substrate of Model I and the constraint Models II and III explain operationally.
- **Garbage Can Model** (Cohen, March, Olsen) — a more radical organizational model where decisions emerge from the accidental confluence of problems, solutions, and participants.
- **Principal-Agent Theory** — the formal economic frame for the conflicts of interest Model III analyzes informally.

## Application Steps

1. State the organizational decision and the puzzling feature that calls for explanation.
2. Apply **Model I**: assume the organization is a rational unitary actor; what goal does the decision optimize, and is that goal the organization's stated goal?
3. Apply **Model II**: what standard operating procedures, routines, or organizational repertoires could have produced this outcome regardless of strategic intent?
4. Apply **Model III**: who were the key players, what were their individual interests, and whose position won the internal bargaining?
5. Compare the three accounts; the most complete explanation typically combines elements of all three with different weights.

## Detection Signals

- A government or corporate decision appears irrational when viewed as a single strategic choice.
- The organization failed to act on clear information that any rational actor would have used.
- Post-mortem analysis of a crisis or failure has stalled at "they made a bad call."
- Official policy and actual behavior diverge in predictable ways that single-actor explanations cannot account for.
- The organization repeatedly produces outputs that no individual leader would have chosen.

## Critical Questions

- Is the entity making the decision actually a complex organization, or an individual? Without organizational structure, Models II and III are vacuous.
- Has Model I been given a fair run, or has the analyst rushed to Model III as a cynical default?
- Does the analyst have sufficient information about the organization's actual procedures (Model II) and internal politics (Model III), or is Model III being constructed speculatively?
- Are the three models being used as parallel projections, or as competing hypotheses where only one can be true?
- Does the combined account explain *more* of the decision's puzzling features than any single model alone? If not, the application has not added analytical value.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Model III monopoly** — every decision is reduced to bureaucratic politics, treating procedure and strategy as irrelevant. Detection: the analyst's accounts read as conspiracies regardless of subject. Correction: re-run Models I and II with the same rigor and weight the accounts by their explanatory power.
- **Model I default** — the analyst stops at the rational-actor account because it is conceptually cleanest. Detection: the puzzling features of the decision remain unexplained but Models II and III were not attempted. Correction: continue to Models II and III explicitly; the puzzling features are typically what those models address.
- **Speculative Model III** — internal politics are constructed without evidence, producing a plausible-sounding but unfalsifiable account. Detection: the Model III narrative cites no documents, witnesses, or specific players. Correction: limit Model III to claims supported by named players and observable allegiances; treat the rest as hypothesis.
- **Combination dodge** — the analyst names all three models without weighting their contributions, producing a non-account that "all three matter." Detection: the conclusion does not specify which model carries most of the explanatory load for which feature. Correction: assign each puzzling feature to the model that best accounts for it.
