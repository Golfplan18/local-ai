# Commitment and Consistency Bias

## Trigger

Invoked from within bias-audit and decision-revision modes when an analyst observes that a past commitment is distorting current evaluation — a project receiving continued funding because it has already received funding, a public position being defended more strongly than it was originally argued, or a course of action being escalated after setbacks rather than reassessed. The host mode supplies the artifact under review (a strategy, decision, or revision proposal); the lens supplies the diagnostic frame and reframing protocol that separates the past commitment from the present forward-looking decision.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Once a person has made a choice, taken a public stand, or invested resources in a direction, internal and social pressures push them to remain consistent with that commitment — even when new evidence indicates they should change course. The drive for consistency is so strong that people unconsciously distort new information to avoid the discomfort of contradiction. Cialdini's principle: prior commitments warp future judgment in ways the committer does not detect.

### Mechanism

Two reinforcing pressures produce the bias. Internally, cognitive dissonance reduction motivates the person to bring beliefs into line with prior actions, since reversing requires admitting the prior action was wrong. Externally, social-identity pressure makes public reversals feel like reputational loss, especially when the original commitment was visible to peers, subordinates, or rivals. Together these pressures produce escalation of commitment: each new investment increases the cost of reversal, which increases the motivation to continue, which generates the next investment.

### Applicability Conditions

- A prior commitment exists that is visible to the decider (a budget allocated, a position stated, a public investment of reputation).
- New evidence has emerged that, evaluated independently, would not support the original commitment.
- The forward-looking decision is being framed as continuation-vs-reversal rather than as a fresh evaluation.
- The decider exhibits one or more of the warning signs in Detection Signals.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating any persistence as commitment bias when in fact the original judgment remains correct under new evidence (the bias diagnosis requires that an unbiased forward-looking evaluation would not select continuation).
- Using the lens to dismiss a difficult course-correction by attributing the original decision to bias when it was actually well-reasoned but unlucky (hindsight bias masquerading as commitment-bias diagnosis).

### Related Models

- **Sunk Cost Fallacy** — the economic counterpart; commitment-consistency is the psychological mechanism that makes sunk-cost reasoning hard to override.
- **Cognitive Dissonance** — the underlying discomfort that consistency-seeking resolves.
- **Escalation of Commitment** — the dynamic pattern produced by repeated commitment-consistency events over time.

## Application Steps

1. Name the prior commitment and the resources or reputation already invested.
2. Reframe the decision as a fresh evaluation: "If I were starting today with no prior investment, would I make this same choice?"
3. Make sunk costs explicit and exclude them from the forward calculation.
4. Create psychological framing for course correction as learning rather than as failure.
5. Decide based solely on forward-looking evidence and expected value.

## Detection Signals

- A project continues to receive resources primarily because it has already received resources.
- The defender of a decision argues more vigorously after the decision is challenged than they did when originally making it.
- A public statement, identity, or stated commitment is making it harder for the person to update.
- Doubling-down behavior follows setbacks rather than reassessment.
- The phrase "we've come too far to stop now" appears in the discussion.

## Critical Questions

- Is the prior commitment actually visible to the decider, or is it being inferred? If inferred, the bias diagnosis is weaker.
- Would a fresh-start evaluation actually reverse the decision, or would it confirm continuation? If continuation is correct under fresh evaluation, the bias is not operative even if commitment exists.
- Is the apparent escalation a rational response to new information that genuinely supports doubling down, or is it consistency-driven distortion of that information?
- Has the decider had access to a structured reframing exercise (e.g., the application steps above), or has the consistency frame dominated all evaluation so far?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Hindsight diagnosis** — Detection signal: the analyst diagnoses commitment bias only after the decision proves wrong. Correction: apply the lens prospectively, not as a post-hoc explanation.
- **Reversal pressure misread as bias** — Detection signal: the analyst pressures reversal of a still-correct decision because the decider has invested in it. Correction: run the fresh-start evaluation independently before invoking the bias frame.
- **Sunk-cost confusion** — Detection signal: the lens is invoked as if synonymous with sunk-cost fallacy without distinguishing the psychological mechanism from the economic error. Correction: name commitment-consistency as the cause and sunk-cost as one of its expressions.
