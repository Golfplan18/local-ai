# Equilibrium

## Trigger

Invoked from within systems-analysis, intervention-design, and market-analysis modes when an analyst is evaluating a stable-looking system to predict whether a planned intervention will be absorbed (stable equilibrium) or will trigger a phase change (unstable equilibrium). The host mode supplies the system and the planned intervention; the lens supplies the stability diagnostic that determines whether to change the underlying forces (for stable equilibria) or to apply the minimal trigger nudge (for unstable equilibria).

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

An equilibrium is a state where opposing forces balance each other so the system has no tendency to change. Stable equilibria resist perturbation and return to their original state when pushed; the intervention is absorbed and produces no lasting change. Unstable equilibria collapse into a new state when nudged; a small intervention triggers cascading shifts. Identifying which type one is in determines the intervention strategy: change the forces (stable) or apply the trigger (unstable).

### Mechanism

In a stable equilibrium, the forces acting on the system are configured such that any displacement generates restoring forces that grow with the displacement, returning the system to its original state. In an unstable equilibrium, the forces are configured such that displacement generates further-displacing forces; a small initial nudge triggers a cascade because each step amplifies the next. The classic physical analog: a ball at the bottom of a bowl (stable) versus a ball balanced on the top of a hill (unstable). In economic and social systems, the underlying forces are typically incentives, habits, network effects, switching costs, and regulation; their configuration determines stability.

### Applicability Conditions

- A market, organization, or system has been static for a meaningful period and the analyst wants to understand why.
- A planned intervention requires predicting whether the system will snap back or shift permanently.
- The forces acting on the system can be identified and characterized as restoring or amplifying.
- The intervention's resource cost is significant enough that misclassifying the equilibrium would be expensive.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating apparent stability as stable equilibrium when the system is actually in a slow-moving transition (frozen-but-not-stable).
- Assuming unstable equilibrium based on a single example of cascading change when the underlying forces would actually produce stability for the planned intervention.

### Related Models

- **Feedback Loops** — equilibria are produced by the interaction of reinforcing and balancing loops.
- **Tipping Point** — the unstable-equilibrium scenario applied to social adoption.
- **Hysteresis** — the related phenomenon where the path between equilibria differs from the reverse path.

## Application Steps

1. Identify the key forces acting on the system and their directions.
2. Determine what is holding the current state in place — incentives, habits, switching costs, regulation, network effects.
3. Assess stability: if the system is pushed slightly, do the forces push it back (stable) or further away (unstable)?
4. To shift a stable equilibrium, change the underlying forces, not just push harder.
5. To exploit an unstable equilibrium, find the minimal nudge that triggers the cascade.

## Detection Signals

- A market or organization has been static for a long time and the reasons are not obvious.
- A planned intervention's success depends on whether the system absorbs or amplifies it.
- Two or more forces are visibly in tension and the balance point matters.
- A small change recently produced a disproportionately large effect — an unstable equilibrium may have tipped.
- The team is trying to dislodge a stuck situation and "pushing harder" is not working.

## Critical Questions

- Has the analyst identified the actual forces holding the system in place, or only described its surface stability?
- Is the equilibrium truly stable, or is it slow-moving transition that looks static on the analyst's time scale?
- For an unstable equilibrium diagnosis, has the cascade mechanism been identified, or is the assessment based on a hopeful analogy to other cases?
- Is the proposed intervention sized appropriately for the diagnosis (force change for stable, minimal trigger for unstable), or is it mismatched?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Push-harder reflex** — Detection signal: facing a stable equilibrium, the team applies more pressure rather than changing the forces; the system absorbs each push. Correction: identify the restoring forces and target them directly.
- **Cascade-hope** — Detection signal: facing what may be a stable equilibrium, the team launches a small intervention hoping for cascading change; the change fails to propagate. Correction: verify unstable-equilibrium structure before relying on cascading effects.
- **Static-vs-slow confusion** — Detection signal: the analyst declares stability when the system is actually transitioning slowly; the diagnosis fails when the slow transition completes. Correction: examine multi-year trends, not just current state.
