# OODA Loop

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that analyze competitive tempo, adaptive execution, or decision cycles under uncertainty — when the question is how fast a team or actor cycles through observation-to-action and how that cycle compares to an adversary's. The host mode supplies the actor, environment, and competitive context; the lens supplies the four-stage cycle and the relative-speed analysis.

## Core Structure

**Input:** A decision-making context with environmental data flow, an actor (individual or team), and one or more counterparties (competitors, adversaries, or environmental dynamics).
**Output:** An assessment of the actor's cycle time relative to counterparties, plus a list of interventions to shorten the actor's loop and lengthen the counterparty's.

1. **Observe.** Gather information from the environment — data, signals, feedback, competitor actions. Input: environmental data flow. Output: a current observation set.

2. **Orient.** Synthesize observations through your mental models, cultural traditions, prior experience, and new information. This is the most critical and most overlooked stage — meaning is made here, and the orientation determines what counts as relevant in subsequent observation. Input: observation set + mental models + cultural and experiential context. Output: a current understanding of the situation.

3. **Decide.** Choose a course of action based on your orientation — commit to a hypothesis. Input: orientation. Output: a decision to act.

4. **Act.** Execute the decision, which changes the environment and generates new observations. Input: decision. Output: an action and the changed environmental state.

5. **Cycle.** Immediately return to step 1 with the new environmental state. The loop never stops; the question is its speed.

6. **Compare cycles.** Estimate the actor's average cycle time and the counterparty's. The faster cycle imposes the slower cycle into a reactive posture.

7. **Intervene on tempo.** Identify levers to shorten the actor's loop (delegation, dashboarding, decision authority at the edge) and to lengthen the counterparty's loop (ambiguity, rapid change, deception, complexity).

## Application Steps

1. Identify the actor and the counterparty whose loops are being compared.
2. Trace one full cycle for each (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act) and estimate elapsed time per stage.
3. Identify the slowest stage in the actor's loop and the most lengthen-able stage in the counterparty's.
4. Apply tempo interventions to both.
5. Re-measure after a defined period.

## Detection Signals

- Speed of decision-making is a competitive advantage.
- The environment is changing rapidly and plans become stale quickly.
- An opponent or competitor is consistently acting faster and the actor is always reacting.
- A team is stuck in analysis paralysis — cycling between Observe and Orient without reaching Decide.
- Decision authority is concentrated far from the action point.

## Critical Questions

- Is the orientation stage actually being performed, or is it being collapsed into observation?
- Are the actor and counterparty operating on the same loop, or different loops with different stakes?
- Does the actor have authority to act, or only to recommend? (Recommendation loops are not OODA loops.)
- Is the speed advantage being squandered by acting on stale orientation?
- Are the counterparty's apparent cycle times misleading because of asynchronous operations?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Orient-skipping** — going from observation to decision without the synthesis that gives observations meaning. Detection: decisions feel reactive and incoherent. Correction: institute a brief, explicit orientation pause even under time pressure.
- **Loop-running theater** — moving fast through the four stages without any actual decisional content. Detection: outputs of cycles look identical to inputs. Correction: require each cycle to produce a decision the prior cycle did not.
- **Tempo-without-quality** — shortening the cycle by degrading the orientation quality. Detection: error rate rises in proportion to cycle-time reduction. Correction: invest in better mental models, not just faster execution.
- **Cycle mismatch** — comparing the actor's tactical cycle against a counterparty's strategic cycle. Detection: the comparison is meaningless because the cycles operate at different scales. Correction: compare cycles at matched scales.
