# Ulrich CSH Boundary Categories

## Trigger

Invoked from within `boundary-critique` (T2) when that mode needs the canonical structure for surfacing the boundary judgments embedded in any system design, plan, or analysis. The host mode supplies the artifact under critique (a plan, policy, design, study, or proposal); the lens supplies the twelve boundary categories — organized in four groups of three — that name the *sources* the analyst must interrogate. Each category is run in is/ought form: what the artifact takes as given (the "is" judgment) versus what it ought to take as given if the affected were to count (the "ought" judgment). The gap between the two is the locus of critique.

## Core Structure

Twelve boundary categories grouped into four sources of design judgment. Each category carries a brief is/ought descriptor and an operational question. The lens is run by interrogating each category in both modes (descriptive: what the artifact actually assumes; normative: what the artifact would assume if those affected had standing) and surfacing the gap.

### Sources of Motivation (whose purposes are served?)

1. **Beneficiary (client).** Whose purposes are being served by the system? *Is*: who does the artifact treat as the beneficiary of its design? *Ought*: who *ought* to be the beneficiary if the affected counted? **Operational question:** Who is (and ought to be) the client of the system?

2. **Purpose.** What should the system bring about? *Is*: what purpose does the artifact pursue as given? *Ought*: what purpose ought the system to serve from the standpoint of the affected? **Operational question:** What is (and ought to be) the purpose of the system?

3. **Measure of improvement.** What counts as success? *Is*: by what measure is improvement assessed in the artifact? *Ought*: what measure ought to define improvement if the affected had voice? **Operational question:** What is (and ought to be) the measure of success?

### Sources of Control (who has decision authority?)

4. **Decision-maker.** Who has decision authority? *Is*: who is positioned by the artifact to decide? *Ought*: who *ought* to have decision authority? **Operational question:** Who is (and ought to be) in command?

5. **Resources.** What resources control execution? *Is*: what resources (and whose) does the artifact draw on? *Ought*: what resources ought to be available, and from where? **Operational question:** What conditions of success are (and ought to be) under the decision-maker's control?

6. **Decision environment.** What does the decision-maker take as given? *Is*: what is treated as outside the decision-maker's control in the artifact? *Ought*: what *ought* to be treated as outside (genuine constraints) versus mistakenly treated as fixed (negotiable)? **Operational question:** What conditions of success are (and ought to be) outside the decision-maker's control?

### Sources of Expertise (who has knowledge to inform the design?)

7. **Expert (planner).** Who has expertise to inform the design? *Is*: whom does the artifact treat as the relevant expert? *Ought*: who *ought* to be treated as having relevant expertise? **Operational question:** Who is (and ought to be) involved as planner?

8. **Expertise.** What expertise is being deployed? *Is*: what knowledge-base does the artifact draw on as authoritative? *Ought*: what knowledge-base ought to be drawn on, including lay, experiential, and indigenous knowledge? **Operational question:** What expertise does (and ought to) inform the design?

9. **Guarantee (guarantor).** What guarantees the design? *Is*: what does the artifact appeal to as guaranteeing that its design will succeed (data, models, expert authority, professional standards)? *Ought*: what ought to be the guarantor, given that all guarantees are partial? **Operational question:** What does (and ought to) guarantee that the planning will succeed?

### Sources of Legitimacy (who speaks for the affected?)

10. **Witness.** Who speaks for those affected but not represented? *Is*: who, if anyone, speaks in the artifact for the affected-but-absent? *Ought*: who *ought* to be witness for the affected-but-absent? **Operational question:** Who is (and ought to be) witness for the interests of those affected but not involved?

11. **Emancipation.** What freedoms or capabilities are at stake for the affected? *Is*: what does the artifact treat as the affected's interests in their own emancipation? *Ought*: what *ought* the affected's emancipatory interests to be treated as? **Operational question:** What are (and ought to be) the chances for the affected to emancipate themselves from the premises of the design?

12. **Worldview.** Whose worldview shapes the analysis? *Is*: what worldview is presupposed by the artifact (often invisibly)? *Ought*: whose worldview *ought* to underpin the design if the affected counted? **Operational question:** What worldview is (and ought to be) determining the design?

The twelve categories together specify the boundary of any system analysis. Boundary critique is the disciplined surfacing of the implicit answers the artifact already gives to all twelve, the alternative answers an *ought* perspective would give, and the consequences of the gap.

## Application Steps

1. Receive the artifact under critique (plan, policy, design, study, proposal) from the host mode.
2. For each of the twelve categories, identify the artifact's *implicit* answer in **is** mode (what the artifact takes as given, even when this is unstated).
3. For each category, generate the *ought* answer from the standpoint of those affected by the artifact (especially the affected-but-not-represented).
4. Surface the gap between is and ought for each category; flag the gaps that are largest, most consequential, or most invisible.
5. Group the gaps into the four source-clusters (motivation, control, expertise, legitimacy) to identify which source of judgment is most contested.
6. Return the twelve-category boundary critique to the host mode, with named gaps and their consequences.

## Detection Signals

- The artifact assumes the beneficiary, purpose, or measure of success without naming them.
- The artifact treats certain conditions as fixed when they are negotiable, or vice versa.
- The artifact appeals to expertise (data, models, professional authority) without surfacing what that expertise excludes.
- The set of decision-makers in the artifact does not include those most affected by its consequences.
- The worldview underpinning the artifact is invisible — it is presented as "the obvious framing" rather than as one framing among possibilities.
- The artifact has stakeholders who are affected-but-not-represented (future generations, non-organized publics, marginalized groups, ecosystems).
- The host mode flags that the artifact's framing seems to foreclose rather than open the analytical space.

## Critical Questions

- Are the *is* answers to all twelve categories surfaced explicitly, or are some left unstated and therefore unexamined?
- Are the *ought* answers generated from the standpoint of the affected, or from the standpoint of the analyst's own values?
- Is the affected-but-not-represented constituency identified, or is the witness category collapsed into "the analyst speaks for them"?
- Is the worldview category interrogated as a worldview, or treated as the natural framing? (The latter is the most common failure.)
- Are the gaps between is and ought reported as boundary judgments under contestation, or as objective findings?
- Does the critique acknowledge that boundary judgments cannot be eliminated — only made visible and contestable — rather than promising a neutral framing?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Implicit-boundary overlook** — only the categories the artifact explicitly addresses are critiqued; implicit answers (often the most consequential) escape examination. Detection: the critique mirrors the structure of the artifact rather than running the full twelve. Correction: force-run all twelve categories regardless of which the artifact addresses.
- **Analyst-substitution for witness** — the analyst speaks for the affected-but-absent without naming that this is what is happening. Detection: the *ought* answers all reflect the analyst's own framing; no actual affected-but-absent constituency is named. Correction: name the witness role explicitly; identify *who* the analyst is standing in for, and what would change if those persons could speak for themselves.
- **Worldview category skipped** — categories 1–11 are run, but worldview (12) is left aside as too philosophical. Detection: the critique surfaces gaps in motivation, control, and expertise but treats the underlying frame as natural. Correction: explicitly run category 12; ask "whose worldview makes these answers seem obvious?"
- **Is/ought collapse** — the categories are run only in is mode (descriptive) or only in ought mode (normative); the gap that is the locus of critique is never produced. Detection: the output is a description of the artifact's assumptions, or a list of normative demands, but not a comparison between the two. Correction: enforce the dual-mode interrogation; the deliverable is the gap, not either side alone.
- **Consensus-seeking framing** — the critique attempts to resolve the gaps rather than surface them, treating boundary judgments as resolvable through dialogue. Detection: the output proposes a "balanced" boundary that elides the disagreement. Correction: report the gaps as live contestation; boundary judgments are political, not technical, and should not be resolved by the analyst alone.

## Source Citations

- Ulrich, Werner (1983). *Critical Heuristics of Social Planning: A New Approach to Practical Philosophy*. Bern: Haupt. The originating text. Develops the twelve boundary categories from Kantian and Habermasian foundations as the structural complement to systems analysis.
- Ulrich, Werner & Reynolds, Martin (2010). "Critical Systems Heuristics." In Reynolds, M. & Holwell, S. (eds.), *Systems Approaches to Managing Change: A Practical Guide*. London: Springer. The accessible operational presentation, with worked examples and the is/ought template.
- Ulrich, Werner (1996). *A Primer to Critical Systems Heuristics for Action Researchers*. University of Hull. Bridges between the philosophical foundations and practitioner application.
- Midgley, Gerald (2000). *Systemic Intervention: Philosophy, Methodology, and Practice*. New York: Kluwer. Extends boundary critique into a broader methodology of systemic intervention; useful when boundary critique must integrate with other systems methods.
