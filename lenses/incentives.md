# Incentives

## Trigger

Invoked from within systems-analysis, organizational-diagnosis, policy-design, behavioral-design, and root-cause-analysis modes when those modes need a named principle that re-anchors explanation away from individual character and onto the reward structure shaping behavior. The host mode supplies a system producing puzzling or undesired outcomes; the lens supplies the discipline of mapping the actual incentive landscape before constructing causal stories about the actors.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Never ask why someone is behaving a certain way until you understand what they are rewarded for. People respond to the incentive they actually face, not the one designers intended. Most organizational dysfunction is an incentive problem disguised as a people problem. The actor-character explanation is almost always a placeholder for an unmapped reward structure; once the structure is mapped, the behavior is rational and the design is the variable to change.

### Mechanism

Behavior is selected by its consequences for the actor — financial, social, status-based, identity-based, or punishment-avoiding. The system rewards what it actually measures and consequences, not what it states it values. If the measured-and-consequenced thing diverges from the intended thing, the actor optimizes for the measured-and-consequenced thing. Second-order effects compound: new incentives generate new gaming strategies; the system's response to gaming generates further adaptation. Treating the actor as the variable produces no improvement; treating the structure as the variable shifts behavior reliably.

### Applicability Conditions

- A system is producing outcomes that puzzle or frustrate its designers.
- People are gaming metrics, optimizing for the wrong thing, or visibly ignoring stated goals.
- The actors in the system are responsive to consequences (not under coercion or extreme constraint).
- The reward structure can be observed (or inferred from records of compensation, promotion, status, sanction).

### Common Misapplications

- Treating incentives as exclusively financial. Status, autonomy, identity, fear of sanction, and approval are all consequential rewards; analyzing only money misses the operative structure.
- Designing new incentives without modeling the gaming response. New metrics generate new optimization strategies; the predictable second-order effect is often the dominant one.
- Treating exhortation, training, or culture work as substitutes for structural change. These can shape values but cannot override consequential rewards over the long run.

### Related Models

- **Goodhart's Law** — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure; the inevitable consequence of measuring what you reward.
- **Principal-agent problem** — the canonical formal frame for misaligned incentives between authority and execution.
- **Cobra effect** — the canonical worked example: rewarding dead cobras generates cobra farming.

### Worked example

A call center rewards agents by calls handled per hour. Quality plummets — agents rush callers off the line, leave problems unresolved, and customers call back repeatedly. The agents are not bad; they are doing exactly what they are paid to do. Fix: tie compensation to first-call resolution rate and customer satisfaction, so the incentive aligns with the actual goal. The behavioral change is rapid and durable; no exhortation about "service quality" was needed.

## Application Steps

1. Receive the system and the puzzling outcome from the host mode.
2. Map the full incentive landscape for each actor: what is each actor actually rewarded for (money, status, approval, autonomy, avoidance of punishment)?
3. Distinguish stated incentives from real incentives — what behavior does the system in fact reward and consequence?
4. Identify the misalignment between desired outcomes and rewarded behaviors.
5. Redesign the incentive so the easiest path for the actor produces the desired outcome.
6. Predict the second-order gaming response and design for it.
7. Return the diagnosis and redesign to the host mode.

## Detection Signals

- A system produces outcomes its designers did not intend and cannot explain.
- People are visibly gaming a metric or optimizing for an obvious proxy of the stated goal.
- The proposed fix is hiring or training rather than restructuring rewards.
- Behavior changed shortly after a compensation or measurement change, in the direction the new measurement rewards.
- The actors describe themselves as doing what the system asks while the designers describe the outcome as failure.

## Critical Questions

- Has the analysis mapped non-financial incentives (status, autonomy, identity, fear of sanction), or only financial ones?
- Has the gaming response to a proposed redesign been modeled? If not, the second-order effect may dominate the first-order improvement.
- Is the proposed change actually consequential, or is it a stated value with no reward attached? Stated values without consequences do not move behavior.
- Has the analysis distinguished between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation? Extrinsic incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation in some contexts; the redesign should anticipate this.
- Are there constraints (legal, ethical, contractual) that the redesign must respect? Some incentive structures are infeasible regardless of their effectiveness.

## Common Failure Modes

- **People-blame substitution** — diagnosing the dysfunction as a talent or character problem when it is an incentive problem. Detection: the proposed fix is firing or hiring rather than restructuring rewards. Correction: re-run the diagnosis explicitly mapping what each actor is rewarded for.
- **Financial-only mapping** — treating money as the only consequential incentive. Detection: the analysis ignores status, autonomy, fear, identity. Correction: map the full reward landscape; financial often turns out not to be the dominant lever.
- **Gaming-blind redesign** — proposing a new metric without modeling how actors will optimize against it. Detection: the redesign assumes actors will not adapt. Correction: explicitly project the gaming response and design for it.
- **Exhortation substitution** — using training, culture work, or values statements in place of structural change. Detection: the intervention is words, the reward structure is unchanged, behavior is expected to improve. Correction: change the reward structure; words follow, not lead.
- **Crowding-out blindness** — replacing intrinsic motivation with financial incentives in contexts where the substitution backfires (creative work, prosocial behavior). Detection: behavior worsened after monetary incentives were introduced. Correction: distinguish contexts where extrinsic incentives complement vs. crowd out intrinsic motivation.
