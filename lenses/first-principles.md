# First Principles Thinking

## Trigger

Invoked from within problem-decomposition, assumption-audit, novel-solution-design, and constraint-verification modes when an analyst is told that a constraint is immovable but its provenance is not verified, when conventional solutions are clunky but no one questions them, or when a market shift may have invalidated old assumptions. The host mode supplies the problem and its currently-accepted constraints; the lens supplies the decomposition protocol that strips inherited convention to bedrock truths, then reconstructs solutions using only those truths as building blocks.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Decompose a problem to the fundamental truths known to be true, then reason upward from those truths. Most everyday reasoning is by analogy — "we do it this way because that is how it has always been done." First-principles thinking strips away convention and inherited assumptions to rebuild understanding from the ground up. It is slower and harder than analogical reasoning, and is the only path to genuinely novel solutions when conventional approaches are constrained by hidden assumptions.

### Mechanism

Two cognitive shifts operate. First, separation: distinguishing physical laws and proven facts (which constrain solutions absolutely) from conventions and inherited assumptions (which constrain only because no one has questioned them). Second, reconstruction: building a solution using only the bedrock-truth set, treating every conventional element as optional unless its necessity can be re-derived. The key insight is that most apparent constraints are conventional rather than physical; the same problem solved from first principles often produces a solution unrecognizable from the conventional approach because the design space is dramatically larger than the conventional view permitted.

### Applicability Conditions

- An existing solution feels clunky, expensive, or inadequate, but no one challenges its core assumptions.
- A constraint is being treated as immovable but its underlying basis (physical law, proven fact, or convention) has not been verified.
- A market or technology shift may have invalidated assumptions that were previously valid.
- The decision-maker has the time and authority to entertain a substantially different solution if the analysis warrants.

### Common Misapplications

- Discarding genuinely physical constraints as "just convention" because the analyst has not understood the physics.
- Using first-principles framing rhetorically to justify pre-determined contrarian conclusions, without actually doing the decomposition work.

### Related Models

- **Falsifiability** — the related epistemic discipline of stating what would make a claim wrong; first-principles asks what would make the constraint mandatory.
- **Inversion** — Charlie Munger's complementary technique: solve the problem by avoiding what would cause failure.
- **Five Whys** — the procedural relative: drill down to root causes rather than to root assumptions.

## Application Steps

1. State the problem or goal clearly.
2. List every assumption baked into the existing approach.
3. For each assumption, ask: is this a physical law, a proven fact, or just convention?
4. Discard everything that is convention; keep only bedrock truths.
5. Reconstruct a solution using only the bedrock truths as building blocks.

## Detection Signals

- Existing solutions feel clunky but no one questions them.
- Someone says "that is just how it works" and the explanation stops there.
- Cost, complexity, or constraints seem immovable but have not been independently verified.
- A market or technology shift has made old assumptions potentially invalid.
- The need to distinguish real constraints from assumed constraints is on the table.

## Critical Questions

- Has the analyst actually done the decomposition work, or is "first principles" being invoked rhetorically to justify a pre-formed conclusion?
- Are the elements identified as "bedrock truths" actually physical laws or proven facts, or are some of them conventions the analyst has accepted?
- Has the reconstructed solution been tested against the original constraints to confirm it solves the actual problem, or only the simplified version?
- Does the decision-maker have the authority and time to act on a substantially different solution? Without that, the analysis is academic.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Rhetorical first-principles** — Detection signal: the framing is invoked but no actual decomposition is shown. Correction: require the assumption list and the bedrock-truth set as outputs.
- **Physics-as-convention** — Detection signal: genuine physical or economic laws are dismissed as convention. Correction: cross-check the bedrock-truth set with domain experts before discarding constraints.
- **Reconstruction ignored** — Detection signal: assumptions are stripped but no replacement solution is built; the analysis stops at deconstruction. Correction: complete the reconstruction step before delivering the analysis.
