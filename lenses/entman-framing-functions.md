# Entman Framing Functions

## Trigger

Invoked from within `frame-audit` (T1) when that mode needs the operational account of what frames *do* in a communication, as distinct from the structural account of what frames *are* (Goffman). The host mode supplies a communication — a news article, speech, document, image, social-media post, policy memo, or other discursive artifact — whose framing is consequential. The lens supplies Entman's four framing functions (define problems, diagnose causes, make moral judgments, suggest remedies) and tests for which of the four are present, which are suppressed, and which combinations produce the artifact's overall framing effect.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

To frame is "to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation." A frame performs (up to) four functions: it **defines problems**, **diagnoses causes**, **makes moral judgments**, and **suggests remedies**. A given communication may perform all four, only some, or only one — but the *combination* of functions performed (and the combinations *not* performed) determines the frame's overall work. The diagnostic task is to identify which functions are present, which are absent, and what alternative functions could have been performed but were not.

### Mechanism

Frames operate through **selection** (what aspects of reality are mentioned) and **salience** (how prominently those aspects are presented — placement, repetition, association with culturally resonant symbols, vivid detail). What is included in a frame and made salient is foregrounded; what is omitted or de-emphasized is backgrounded. Importantly, *omission* is as much a framing operation as inclusion: a problem definition that omits causes operates differently than one that supplies them, even if both are technically "neutral."

The four functions interact systemically. A frame that defines a problem (function 1) without diagnosing causes (function 2) typically directs attention to *symptoms* and forecloses structural analysis. A frame that diagnoses causes without making moral judgments (function 3) typically presents the situation as technical and apolitical. A frame that makes moral judgments without suggesting remedies (function 4) typically generates outrage without action. The most consequential frames in political communication often perform all four functions in tight integration: a problem is defined (X is happening), a cause is named (Y group is responsible), a moral judgment is made (Y is blameworthy), and a remedy is suggested (Y must be restricted) — producing a self-reinforcing whole that closes the analytical space against alternative framings.

Crucially, *frames operate outside the awareness of audiences and often outside the awareness of communicators*. A journalist may not consciously frame; the frame emerges from selection conventions, source reliance, deadline pressure, audience expectations, and institutional position. Detecting frames requires attention not to communicator intent but to the *structure of selection and salience* in the artifact itself.

### Applicability Conditions

- The artifact is a communication — text, speech, image, video, or composite — that addresses a contested situation or condition.
- The communication's effect depends on which problem-definition, cause, moral-judgment, and/or remedy it foregrounds.
- The communication is part of a competitive discursive field where alternative framings are or could be in circulation.
- The host mode needs to identify what the artifact does framingly, beyond reporting what it says propositionally.
- The communication is being analyzed for either its rhetorical effect, its ideological commitments, or its omissions relative to alternative framings.

### Common Misapplications

- **Function-tally substitution.** Counting how many of the four functions are present, as if quantitative coverage measured framing quality. The diagnostic value is in *which combinations* are present and what is gained or lost by them, not in the count.
- **Omission-blindness.** Cataloguing only what the frame *contains*, missing the framing work of what is omitted. A problem definition without a cause-diagnosis is doing framing work precisely by omitting; the omission must be surfaced.
- **Intent-as-frame.** Equating the frame with the communicator's intent, and treating unintentional framing as not really framing. Frames operate through selection-and-salience patterns regardless of intent; the frame is in the artifact, not in the head behind it.
- **Single-frame description.** Describing the frame in the artifact as if it were the only possible framing, without surfacing the alternative framings against which the chosen frame's selections-and-salience are operating.

### Related Models

- **Goffman frame analysis** — structural complement; where Entman names the *functions* a frame performs in communication, Goffman provides the *structural mechanics* of how frames organize subjective experience. Use both: Goffman for what a frame *is*, Entman for what it *does*.
- **Lakoff frames** — overlapping; Lakoff foregrounds the cognitive-semantic frames activated by particular words and metaphors. Entman's functions and Lakoff's cognitive-frames are compatible; Lakoff supplies the lexical/cognitive substrate, Entman supplies the functional taxonomy of what a frame accomplishes.
- **Stanley propaganda** — connects framing to undemocratic ideology; Entman's framing functions are the operational mechanism through which Stanley's flawed ideologies spread.
- **Iyengar episodic vs. thematic framing** — extends Entman; episodic frames focus on individual instances (which obscure cause-diagnosis), thematic frames focus on broader trends (which support cause-diagnosis).
- **Snow & Benford collective action frames** — applies Entman's logic to social-movement framing (diagnostic, prognostic, motivational frames).

## Application Steps

1. Receive the communication artifact from the host mode.
2. Identify what aspects of reality the artifact has *selected* (made present) and which it has *omitted*; note both.
3. Identify the *salience* devices in play (placement, repetition, vivid detail, cultural-symbol association, source-of-quote).
4. For each of the four functions, ask:
   - **Define problems** — What does the artifact treat as the problem? What conditions are characterized as problematic, for whom, with what costs?
   - **Diagnose causes** — What does the artifact identify as causing the problem? Are causes named at all? If named, are they structural, individual, accidental, intentional?
   - **Make moral judgments** — Who or what does the artifact treat as blameworthy or praiseworthy? Whose suffering is foregrounded? Whose is omitted?
   - **Suggest remedies** — What action does the artifact suggest, explicitly or implicitly? What range of remedies does it foreclose?
5. Surface the *alternative framings* — for each function, what other problem-definitions, causes, moral judgments, and remedies could have been foregrounded but were not?
6. Return the four-function audit + alternative-framing inventory to the host mode.

## Detection Signals

- The artifact addresses a contested issue where multiple framings are in circulation in the broader discourse.
- The host mode flags that the artifact's persuasive effect depends on selection and salience, not just on its propositional content.
- The artifact treats one problem-definition, cause, judgment, or remedy as the natural framing without acknowledging alternatives.
- The artifact systematically omits one or more of the four functions (the omissions themselves carry framing weight).
- The communication is being deployed as part of a strategic effort to shape public understanding (campaigns, advocacy materials, policy advocacy).
- The artifact uses culturally resonant symbols, vivid imagery, or repetition patterns that suggest deliberate salience-management.

## Critical Questions

- Have all four functions been examined, including those *not* performed by the artifact? Omission is framing.
- Have the *alternative framings* been surfaced for each function — what other problem-definitions, causes, moral judgments, and remedies could the artifact have foregrounded but did not?
- Is the analysis attentive to *how* salience is achieved (placement, repetition, vividness, source-quotation, symbol-association), not just to what is selected?
- Has the analyst checked the artifact's framing against the framings circulating in adjacent media or earlier coverage of the same issue? A frame that appears natural in isolation may be revealed as one option among many in comparison.
- Is the framing analysis treating *intent* as the frame's source, when the frame may be operating through institutional convention, source reliance, or unexamined cultural defaults?
- Does the analysis acknowledge that the analyst's own framing of the artifact is itself a frame, with its own four-function structure?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Two-function reduction** — the analyst identifies the problem-definition and the suggested-remedy but skips diagnosis-of-cause and moral-judgment. Detection: the analysis describes "what the article says is happening and what it recommends," missing the causal and evaluative framing. Correction: force-run all four functions, including the ones the artifact most strongly omits.
- **Omission-blindness** — the analyst catalogs what the frame contains and treats absence as analytical irrelevance. Detection: the analysis would not change if a major omission were filled in. Correction: explicitly enumerate, for each function, what the artifact has omitted that competing framings include.
- **Intent-substitution** — the analyst attributes the frame to the communicator's deliberate strategy, ignoring framing produced by institutional defaults, source dependence, audience expectation, or cultural convention. Detection: the analysis converges on what the communicator *meant*, not on what the artifact *does*. Correction: treat the frame as a property of the artifact's selection-and-salience pattern, not of any single intentional act.
- **Single-frame naturalization** — the analyst describes the artifact's framing as if it were the only available framing, missing the field of alternatives against which the artifact's choices are doing work. Detection: the analysis lacks any reference to how the same situation is framed differently elsewhere. Correction: locate the artifact in its discursive field; surface the alternative framings the artifact is suppressing or competing against.
- **Function-tally fetish** — the analyst counts how many of the four functions are present and reports the count as the diagnostic. Detection: the analysis output is "this article performs three of the four functions." Correction: the diagnostic value is in which combinations are present, what work each combination does, and what alternative combinations would do differently — not in the count.

## Source Citations

- Entman, Robert M. (1993). "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm." *Journal of Communication* 43(4):51-58. The originating article. Defines framing as selection + salience and articulates the four functions.
- Entman, Robert M. (2004). *Projections of Power: Framing News, Public Opinion, and U.S. Foreign Policy*. University of Chicago Press. Develops cascading-activation model of how frames spread from administration to media to public.
- Iyengar, Shanto (1991). *Is Anyone Responsible? How Television Frames Political Issues*. University of Chicago Press. Extends Entman with the episodic/thematic distinction; demonstrates that episodic framing systematically obscures cause-diagnosis.
- Snow, David A. & Benford, Robert D. (1988). "Ideology, Frame Resonance, and Participant Mobilization." *International Social Movement Research* 1:197-217. Operationalizes Entman's logic for social movements; develops diagnostic/prognostic/motivational frame distinction.
- Reese, Stephen D., Gandy, Oscar H. & Grant, August E. (eds.) (2001). *Framing Public Life: Perspectives on Media and Our Understanding of the Social World*. Lawrence Erlbaum. Comprehensive volume situating Entman's contribution within the broader framing-research field.
