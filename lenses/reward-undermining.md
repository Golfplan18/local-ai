# Reward Undermining

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that design incentives, compensation, or recognition systems for activities people already perform with intrinsic motivation — open-source contribution, creative work, volunteer effort, professional craft. The host mode supplies the activity, the actors, and the proposed external reward; the lens supplies the crowding-out analysis that warns when the reward will displace rather than augment intrinsic motivation.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

External incentives — especially monetary ones — can destroy intrinsic motivation. Also called the overjustification effect or motivation crowding, this phenomenon occurs when people shift their mental framing from "I do this because I find it meaningful" to "I do this because I get paid for it." Once that shift occurs, removing the reward does not restore the original motivation; instead, the activity feels like uncompensated labor. Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, and relatedness drive intrinsic motivation, and extrinsic rewards undermine the sense of autonomy.

### Mechanism

When an activity is intrinsically motivated, the actor's frame is "I choose to do this." Adding a contingent external reward shifts the perceived locus of causation from internal (autonomous) to external (controlled). The frame becomes "I do this because I am paid." This frame persists once established and converts the activity from an expression of autonomy into a transaction. Removing the reward removes the transactional motivation but does not restore the autonomy frame; the actor now experiences the unpaid activity as exploitation.

### Applicability Conditions

- The activity has existing intrinsic motivation.
- The proposed reward is contingent (paid for doing X) rather than unconditional.
- The activity has identity or meaning content for the actors.
- The reward is large enough to register as the framing for the activity.

### Common Misapplications

- Avoiding all external rewards, including those for genuinely uninteresting work where they would be effective.
- Treating recognition the same as monetary contingent reward; non-contingent recognition often does not crowd out.
- Failing to distinguish unexpected bonuses (low crowding) from contingent payment (high crowding).
- Assuming all populations respond identically; cultural and individual variation is real.

### Related Models

- **Self-Determination Theory** — the broader theoretical frame.
- **Goal Substitution** — adjacent failure: external metrics displace true goals.
- **Procedural Justice** — what makes rewards feel autonomy-supporting rather than controlling.

### Worked example

An open-source project has active volunteer contributors who submit patches out of genuine interest. The project receives funding and decides to pay contributors per accepted patch. Initially, contributions spike. Within six months, the most prolific volunteers have either left or reduced output. The payment reframed contributions from gifts to transactions, and the rates felt insultingly low compared to consulting fees. New contributors negotiate rates before starting. The community shifted from gift economy to market economy, and the original motivation was crowded out.

## Application Steps

1. Before adding an external incentive, ask: Is there existing intrinsic motivation here? If yes, proceed with extreme caution.
2. For tasks that are inherently interesting or meaningful, prefer intrinsic motivators — increase autonomy, provide mastery opportunities, connect work to purpose.
3. If external rewards are necessary, make them unexpected rather than contingent ("here's a bonus because you did great work" vs. "you'll get a bonus if you hit this target").
4. For tasks that are genuinely uninteresting and have no intrinsic appeal, external incentives work fine — the risk only applies when there is intrinsic motivation to crowd out.
5. Never remove an established external reward without replacing it with something — the intrinsic motivation is already gone and removal creates resentment, not restoration.

## Detection Signals

- The activity currently runs on volunteer or intrinsic energy.
- A bonus, prize, or gamification system is being proposed.
- Volunteer contributions have dropped after a payment system was introduced.
- A creative team has become mechanical and output-driven after incentive targets were set.
- The actors describe the activity in identity terms ("I am a maker") rather than transactional terms.

## Critical Questions

- Is there genuine intrinsic motivation to protect, or is the analyst romanticizing reluctant labor?
- Will the proposed reward be perceived as autonomy-supporting (recognition of choice) or controlling (compensation for compliance)?
- Is the magnitude of the reward large enough to register as the activity's framing?
- Have non-monetary alternatives been considered (autonomy, mastery, purpose)?
- What is the exit cost if the experiment fails?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Premature monetization** — adding contingent payment to volunteer activity. Detection: volunteer engagement drops post-payment. Correction: shift to non-contingent funding (infrastructure, conferences, recognition).
- **Reward removal** — withdrawing established external rewards expecting intrinsic motivation to return. Detection: removal produces resentment, not voluntarism. Correction: maintain rewards once established; design carefully before introducing.
- **Contingency confusion** — failing to distinguish unexpected bonuses from contingent payment. Detection: well-intended unexpected bonuses get rationalized as contingent over time. Correction: communicate clearly and avoid pattern-recognition that makes unexpected feel contingent.
