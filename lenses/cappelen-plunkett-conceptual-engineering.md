# Cappelen-Plunkett Conceptual Engineering

## Trigger

Invoked from within `conceptual-engineering` (T10) when that mode needs the framework distinguishing descriptive from engineering modes of conceptual analysis and the apparatus for proposing, justifying, and assessing concept revisions. The host mode supplies a target concept, the analytical situation that motivates revising it, and the population whose use of the concept is at issue; the lens supplies the philosophical apparatus (Cappelen's master argument, Haslanger's amelioration, the implementation problem, the descriptive/engineering distinction) that structures the engineering proposal.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

A concept is not a fixed feature of the world to be discovered but an artifact of human cognition and communication that can be evaluated, criticized, and revised on the basis of how well it serves our purposes. Conceptual analysis comes in two modes: the *descriptive* mode asks what the concept currently is (what its extension, intension, or inferential role actually is in current usage); the *engineering* mode asks what the concept *should* be given the purposes the concept is meant to serve. The two modes answer different questions and engaging the wrong one produces talking-past confusions.

### Mechanism

Cappelen's master argument for conceptual engineering runs as follows: (1) our concepts are defective in various ways — they fail to track what we want them to track, they encode false presuppositions, they obscure morally significant distinctions, or they are simply unsuited to the purposes for which they are used; (2) we can sometimes do better by revising the concept rather than by adjusting the world or accepting the defective concept; (3) such revision is therefore a legitimate and sometimes obligatory intellectual project. The argument is structurally similar to engineering critique in any other domain: when a tool is ill-suited to its task, the response is to redesign the tool, not to redesign the task or accept the poor fit.

The methodology Haslanger developed independently in feminist philosophy under the name *ameliorative analysis* operationalizes this. An ameliorative analysis of a concept C asks: what work do we want C to do for us? Given those purposes, what is the best version of C? The output is a proposed revised concept that better serves the identified purposes, with explicit acknowledgment that the proposal departs from current usage and an argument for why the departure is warranted. Haslanger's worked examples (gender, race) demonstrate the move: rather than asking "what is gender?" descriptively, ask "what should gender mean given the political and analytical purposes our talk of gender is meant to serve?" The answer is a proposed concept (gender as a hierarchical social position grounded in perceived biological features) that may diverge from how the term is currently used but better serves the analytical purpose.

The engineering mode raises an immediate puzzle: the *implementation problem*. Even if a revised concept would be better, how does an individual analyst (or even a community) actually shift the concept's usage in the broader population? Concepts are public artifacts shaped by countless speakers and writers; an individual proposal does not, by itself, change usage. Cappelen's view is broadly skeptical: implementation is largely outside the engineer's control, and the engineering project is justified independently of whether the proposed revision is taken up. Other theorists (notably Sterken, Plunkett, others) argue that the implementation problem is more tractable than Cappelen suggests and that engineering proposals can succeed when they are well-positioned (academic terminology, legal definitions, professional vocabulary). The status of the implementation problem is a live debate, surfaced as a critical question below.

The descriptive/engineering distinction is the lens's central operational move. Many philosophical and political disputes that look like factual disagreements are really disagreements about which mode is being engaged. When one party says "X is a Y" and another says "X is not a Y," they may be (a) descriptively disagreeing about current usage; (b) engineering-disagreeing about what the concept should be; or (c) one in each mode, talking past each other. Diagnosing which dispute is at hand is the first step in productive engagement.

### Applicability Conditions

- The host mode's question concerns a concept whose usage or content is contested.
- The analytical situation is one where the contestation is not just about facts but about what a concept should mean.
- A revision is being proposed (or could be), and the proposal needs justification grounded in the concept's purposes.
- The population whose usage matters can be identified (academic discourse, legal practice, broader public, etc.) so the implementation problem can be addressed.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating an engineering proposal as a descriptive claim. "Gender should mean X" is an engineering proposal; "gender currently means X in usage Y" is a descriptive claim. Conflating the two produces both bad descriptions (asserting as factual what is in fact a proposal) and bad engineering (defending engineering proposals by appeal to descriptive accuracy).
- Treating a descriptive claim as an engineering proposal. When someone reports the actual usage of a concept, refusing to accept the report because it conflicts with one's preferred usage is a category mistake.
- Ignoring the implementation problem. Proposing a concept revision without addressing how (or whether) it could be implemented in the relevant population produces an analytically interesting but pragmatically inert proposal.
- Treating Haslanger's ameliorative method as the only conceptual engineering method. Haslanger's approach is one well-developed instance; Cappelen, Plunkett, Sterken, Burgess, Eklund, and others have proposed alternative methodological frames with different commitments about what makes a revision good.
- Engineering by personal preference. The methodology requires identifying purposes the concept serves and arguing from those purposes; "I prefer this version of the concept" is not an engineering argument.

### Related Models

- **Gallie essentially contested concepts** — concepts whose usage is permanently disputed because the dispute is internal to the concept's normative point. Conceptual engineering can target essentially contested concepts but must reckon with the contestation as a feature, not a bug.
- **Haslanger ameliorative analysis** — a specific engineering methodology grounded in identifying the political and analytical work the concept is meant to do.
- **Walton schemes (Argument from Commitment)** — when an engineering proposal targets a concept the audience is publicly committed to, the audience's prior commitments structure the dialectic.
- **Lakoff conceptual metaphor** — concepts often carry metaphorical structure that constrains their revisability; engineering must reckon with the metaphorical infrastructure.
- **Stanley propaganda** — undermining propaganda often works by engineering concepts in the opposite direction (eroding rather than improving), making engineering analysis dual-use.

## Application Steps

1. Receive the target concept and analytical situation from the host mode.
2. Diagnose whether the host's question is descriptive or engineering; if both are in play, separate them.
3. If descriptive: report current usage, intension, extension, and inferential role; flag dimensions on which usage is unstable or contested.
4. If engineering: identify the purposes the concept is meant to serve; propose a revised concept that better serves those purposes; argue from purposes to proposal.
5. Address the implementation problem: identify the population whose usage is at issue; assess feasibility of uptake; document residual implementation gap if uptake is uncertain.
6. Distinguish engineering proposal from descriptive claim in the report; do not let the engineering output read as if it were a description of current usage.
7. Return the descriptive analysis, the engineering proposal (where applicable), the purpose-justification, the implementation assessment, and the descriptive/engineering distinction to the host mode.

## Detection Signals

- A concept's usage is contested and the contestation is not resolvable by appeal to facts alone.
- The host mode is being asked whether a concept "should" mean X — a normative-conceptual question rather than a descriptive one.
- An ameliorative or reformist project (in feminist, anti-racist, legal, or scientific contexts) is proposing a concept revision and the proposal needs philosophical structuring.
- Two parties are arguing about a concept and appear to be talking past each other — a candidate diagnosis is that one is in descriptive mode and the other in engineering mode.
- Mid-analysis, the analyst notices that an argument's force depends on adopting a specific (revised or contested) version of a concept rather than the version in current usage.

## Critical Questions

- Is this a descriptive dispute, an engineering dispute, or one of each? Misdiagnosing the mode produces talking-past confusions.
- What purposes is the concept meant to serve? Without identified purposes, an engineering proposal has no ground for justification.
- Does the proposed revision actually serve those purposes better than the current concept? An engineering proposal that fails to demonstrate this is unmotivated.
- How will the revised concept be implemented in the relevant population? The implementation problem is live; ignoring it leaves the proposal pragmatically inert. (Note: this question's status as a defeater vs. a separate concern is itself debated — Cappelen would say implementation does not undermine the engineering project's legitimacy; others disagree.)
- Does the revision generate semantic discontinuity that defeats its purpose? If the revised concept is so different from the original that audiences hear a different word, the engineering may have produced a homonym rather than a revision.
- Is the proposal being defended by appeal to current usage (a descriptive consideration) or by appeal to purposes (an engineering consideration)? Mixing the two is a category mistake; engineering proposals are defended by purpose-fit, not by descriptive accuracy.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Mode confusion** — engaging an engineering question as a descriptive one or vice versa. Detection: the parties are arguing past each other; descriptive evidence is offered against an engineering proposal or normative argument is offered against a descriptive claim. Correction: explicitly diagnose the mode each party is engaging; restructure the dialogue around the diagnosed modes.
- **Implementation ignored** — proposing a concept revision without addressing how it would be taken up in usage. Detection: the proposal is methodologically rigorous but the analyst cannot say what would happen if it were "adopted." Correction: identify the population, the institutional levers (academic, legal, professional), and the realistic uptake horizon; document the residual implementation gap.
- **Purpose-vacuum engineering** — proposing a revision without identifying the purposes the concept serves. Detection: the proposal reads as personal preference or aesthetic judgment rather than engineering. Correction: identify the purposes; argue from purposes to proposal; if no purposes can be identified, the engineering project does not have the materials it needs.
- **Strawman of current usage** — defending an engineering proposal by misdescribing current usage to make the revision look obviously needed. Detection: the descriptive claim about current usage does not survive scrutiny by competent users of the concept. Correction: get the descriptive claim right first; then argue for revision against the accurate description.
- **Neutrality theatre** — presenting an engineering proposal as a descriptive observation to bypass the audience's right to evaluate the proposal qua proposal. Detection: the report says "X is Y" when the supporting argument is purposive rather than descriptive. Correction: re-cast the claim explicitly as a proposal; let the audience evaluate it against alternatives.
