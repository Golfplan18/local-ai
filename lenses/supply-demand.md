# Supply and Demand

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that explain or predict prices, salaries, valuations, or attention-allocation — when the question is why a value is what it is or where it is heading. The host mode supplies the good or resource and the market context; the lens supplies the supply-demand intersection analysis that decomposes price into the contributions of each side.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Price is not intrinsic to a thing — it emerges from the interaction of how much is available (supply) and how much people want it (demand). When supply exceeds demand, prices fall; when demand exceeds supply, prices rise. This applies far beyond money: attention, talent, status, and time all follow the same dynamic. Understanding which side of the equation is shifting explains most market behavior.

### Mechanism

In a competitive market, suppliers offer more at higher prices (upward-sloping supply) and demanders buy more at lower prices (downward-sloping demand). The equilibrium price is the intersection. When supply shifts (production cost change, new entrants, scarcity), the equilibrium shifts along the demand curve. When demand shifts (preferences, income, substitutes), the equilibrium shifts along the supply curve. Decomposing observed price changes requires identifying which curve shifted.

### Applicability Conditions

- A market or market-like exchange exists.
- Supply and demand can be at least roughly characterized.
- Price is not administratively fixed in a way that prevents adjustment.
- The good is reasonably well-defined.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating short-run price movements as if equilibrium logic applied (it takes time to reach equilibrium).
- Ignoring that supply and demand can shift simultaneously.
- Confusing change in quantity demanded (movement along curve) with change in demand (curve shift).
- Applying the model to markets with severe information asymmetry, monopoly, or coordination failure without adjustment.

### Related Models

- **Scarcity** — what shifts the supply curve to the left.
- **Niches** — strategic positioning to enter the demand curve where competition is thin.
- **Equilibrium** — the broader concept supply-demand equilibrium instantiates.

### Worked example

In 2020, remote-work software engineers were in massive demand with constrained supply. Salaries spiked. By 2024, supply grew (bootcamp graduates, AI-assisted coding, offshore talent) while some demand contracted (hiring freezes, AI replacing junior tasks). Salaries flattened or fell for generalist roles. Neither situation was a mystery — the supply-demand balance simply shifted, and compensation followed.

## Application Steps

1. Identify the specific good, service, or resource in question.
2. Map the supply side — who provides it, how much exists, what are the barriers to producing more?
3. Map the demand side — who wants it, how badly, and what substitutes exist?
4. Identify which side is shifting and in which direction.
5. Predict the price or value trajectory based on the direction of the shift.

## Detection Signals

- A price, salary, or valuation is changing and an explanation is needed.
- Entering a market and assessing whether the offering will command a premium or face commoditization.
- Talent markets — explaining why certain skills are highly compensated.
- Evaluating whether a business model depends on a supply-demand imbalance that may not last.
- Strategic decisions about when to build, buy, or wait.

## Critical Questions

- Has the analyst correctly identified which curve shifted, supply or demand?
- Is the shift permanent or transient?
- Are there frictions (regulation, coordination failure) that prevent equilibrium adjustment?
- What substitutes exist, and how does their availability affect the demand curve's shape?
- How long until new entrants or shifting demand restore equilibrium?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Curve confusion** — treating curve shifts and movements along curves as equivalent. Detection: explanation conflates "demand increased" with "quantity demanded increased." Correction: distinguish carefully.
- **Static-equilibrium application** — using equilibrium logic during dynamic adjustment. Detection: prediction misses overshoots and lags. Correction: incorporate adjustment dynamics.
- **Friction blindness** — applying competitive-market logic to markets with severe friction. Detection: prices fail to adjust as predicted. Correction: identify the friction and model it explicitly.
