# Pareto Principle

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that allocate scarce attention or resources across many candidates — prioritization, root-cause analysis, customer-segmentation, defect triage — when effort is being distributed evenly but results are uneven. The host mode supplies the candidates and the outcome metric; the lens supplies the unequal-distribution expectation that surfaces the vital few.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. Vilfredo Pareto observed that 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of the population; the pattern recurs across business, software, health, and daily life. The exact ratio varies, but the core insight is that inputs are not equally productive. A small number of causes, customers, features, or efforts typically drive a disproportionate share of results. Focus on the vital few, not the trivial many.

### Mechanism

Many natural and human-system distributions are heavy-tailed (power-law-like) rather than normal. In heavy-tailed distributions, a small fraction of observations accounts for most of the cumulative magnitude. This is generated by reinforcing dynamics (rich-get-richer), preferential attachment, multiplicative processes, and threshold effects. The 80/20 ratio is approximate; the underlying pattern is heavy-tailedness, which can produce 90/10 or 70/30 in different contexts.

### Applicability Conditions

- The candidate set has enough members for a distributional pattern to emerge (typically dozens or more).
- The outcome metric is meaningful and measurable per candidate.
- The candidates are causally separable (one's contribution doesn't entirely depend on others).
- The cost of measurement is low relative to the prioritization payoff.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating 80/20 as a precise number rather than a heuristic for heavy-tailedness.
- Applying the principle in domains where contributions are interdependent and cannot be ranked individually.
- Cutting the trivial many indiscriminately when some are necessary substrate for the vital few.
- Re-running the analysis on the remaining 80% and discovering "another 80/20" — true, but trivially so, not a fresh insight.

### Related Models

- **Bottlenecks** — the vital few may be vital because they sit at constraint points.
- **Diminishing Returns** — why effort allocation should track marginal contribution, not equal share.
- **Long Tail** — the inverse insight: in some domains, the cumulative trivial many exceeds the vital few.

### Worked example

A product team tracks customer support tickets and finds that 4 of their 22 features generate 78% of all complaints. Instead of spreading the engineering team across general improvements, they focus an entire sprint on those 4 features. Support ticket volume drops by 60%, customer satisfaction scores rise, and the team freed up capacity that would have been wasted on low-impact polish elsewhere.

## Application Steps

1. Identify the outcome you care about (revenue, bugs, satisfaction, learning).
2. List all inputs or contributing factors.
3. Measure or estimate each input's contribution to the outcome.
4. Rank by contribution — find the 20% (roughly) that drives 80% of the result.
5. Allocate disproportionate resources to the vital few; reduce, delegate, or eliminate the trivial many.
6. Re-evaluate periodically — the vital few shift over time.

## Detection Signals

- Effort is distributed evenly across tasks but results are uneven.
- Resources are limited and prioritization is essential.
- A small subset of inputs (customers, bugs, features) seems to drive most outcomes.
- Perfectionism or completionism is slowing progress on what matters most.
- Triage is needed — not everything can be done, so what yields the most?

## Critical Questions

- Are the contributions actually independent, or does the apparent 20% depend on the rest of the substrate?
- Is the metric chosen the right one, or is the 80/20 distribution an artifact of measurement?
- What changes when the analysis is re-run — does the vital 20% remain stable or shift?
- Are some "trivial many" items strategically important despite low contribution to the chosen metric?
- Is the analyst applying the principle as insight or as ritual?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Decimal-point fetishism** — insisting on precise 80/20 when the underlying distribution may be 70/30 or 90/10. Detection: time spent debating exact percentages. Correction: focus on the heavy-tailedness, not the specific number.
- **Substrate amputation** — cutting the trivial many that turn out to be necessary for the vital few. Detection: vital-few performance degrades after trivial-many cuts. Correction: identify dependencies before cutting.
- **Recursive Pareto** — repeatedly applying 80/20 to the residual, claiming new insight each time. Detection: the analysis produces nothing new beyond mathematical truism. Correction: stop after the first cut and act on it.
