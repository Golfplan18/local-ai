# Reciprocity

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that analyze influence, alliance-building, negotiation, or unsolicited generosity — when a gift, concession, or favor is creating obligation that may or may not be proportionate. The host mode supplies the exchange context and the parties involved; the lens supplies the obligation-mechanism analysis that distinguishes genuine relationship-building from manipulation.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Humans feel a deep, automatic obligation to return favors, gifts, and concessions — and to punish those who take without giving back. This instinct is ancient and cross-cultural; it is the foundation of trade, alliance, and social cohesion. But it is also easily exploited: an unsolicited small gift creates a sense of obligation that can be leveraged into a disproportionate return.

### Mechanism

Reciprocity is enforced by social emotions (gratitude, indignation) that evolved to support cooperation in repeated interactions. When activated, these emotions operate semi-automatically and can override rational evaluation of the actual exchange ratio. Manipulators exploit the mechanism by giving small, unsolicited gifts that activate gratitude without the recipient consciously evaluating proportionality. Defense requires conscious examination of whether the obligation matches the exchange.

### Applicability Conditions

- An exchange or potential exchange exists between parties.
- One party has acted first, creating a reciprocity expectation.
- The parties are in a context where reciprocity norms apply (most human contexts).
- The proportionality of exchange can be evaluated.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating all gifts as manipulation when many are genuine.
- Ignoring reciprocity when building genuine relationships.
- Over-correcting into transactional accounting that destroys trust.
- Applying the lens to detect manipulation while ignoring its constructive use.

### Related Models

- **Tit for Tat** — the strategic version: condition future cooperation on past reciprocity.
- **Commitment and Consistency** — adjacent influence mechanism that compounds with reciprocity.
- **Social Proof** — adjacent influence mechanism that operates on a different channel.

### Worked example

A vendor sends your team an expensive holiday gift basket and follows up the next week asking you to renew a contract at unfavorable terms. The gift was not generosity — it was a reciprocity trigger designed to make saying no feel socially uncomfortable. Recognizing the mechanism lets you evaluate the contract on its merits. Conversely, when you want to build a genuine relationship with a new partner, offering early value (introductions, shared data, helpful feedback) with no immediate ask creates organic reciprocity that compounds over time.

## Application Steps

1. Recognize the reciprocity trigger — did someone give you something that creates a sense of obligation?
2. Evaluate whether the exchange is proportionate — a small gift should not obligate a large return.
3. In negotiations, make measured concessions and expect reciprocation — but do not over-concede early.
4. To build trust, go first with a genuine, low-cost gesture — but track whether it is reciprocated.
5. When reciprocity breaks down, name the pattern explicitly rather than escalating silently.

## Detection Signals

- Someone offers an unexpected favor, gift, or concession and then makes a request.
- An exchange feels socially obligating despite the analyst not initiating it.
- A negotiation involves sequential concessions where each side expects matching.
- Trust has broken down and tit-for-tat retaliation is escalating.
- A relationship is being built or eroded based on perceived give-take balance.

## Critical Questions

- Is the gift proportionate to the requested return?
- Was the gift solicited or imposed?
- Is the relationship genuinely mutual, or is one party always the giver?
- Would the analyst's response change if the gift had not been given?
- Is the manipulation hypothesis grounded in pattern, or in single-event paranoia?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Reciprocity blindness** — failing to recognize manipulation. Detection: recurring disproportionate returns to small gifts. Correction: explicitly evaluate exchange ratios before responding.
- **Cynical over-correction** — treating all generosity as manipulation. Detection: relationships fail to develop because every gift is suspect. Correction: distinguish solicited proportionate exchange from unsolicited disproportionate.
- **Transactional accounting** — tracking exchanges so precisely that the relationship becomes mercenary. Detection: trust erodes despite balanced exchange. Correction: maintain general balance over time, not per-exchange.
