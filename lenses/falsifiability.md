# Falsifiability

## Trigger

Invoked from within hypothesis-evaluation, claim-testing, and theory-rigor modes when an analyst is evaluating whether a claim is empirically meaningful — when a theory appears to explain everything, when proponents have an answer for every contradicting observation, or when no conceivable evidence could prove a statement wrong. The host mode supplies the claim, theory, or model under evaluation; the lens supplies Popper's demarcation criterion and the prospective-test protocol that requires defining in advance what observation would falsify the claim.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

A statement is empirically meaningful only if some conceivable observation could prove it wrong. If a theory is compatible with every possible outcome, it predicts nothing and explains nothing. Popper proposed falsifiability as the demarcation criterion separating science from non-science: "A theory which is not refutable by any conceivable event is non-scientific." The strongest version of a theory is the one that takes the most risk — that forbids the most possible observations.

### Mechanism

Falsifiability functions through three sub-properties. Specificity: a precise claim is more falsifiable than a vague one because it forecloses more possible observations. Bridge to observation: a falsifiable claim must connect to observable consequences, not just to other concepts. Refutation acceptance: a genuinely falsifiable theory carries a commitment to abandon or revise the theory when the falsifying observation occurs, rather than to add ad-hoc auxiliary hypotheses ("rescue clauses") that protect the theory at the cost of its predictive content. Theories that survive only by accumulating rescue clauses become unfalsifiable in practice even if originally falsifiable in form.

### Applicability Conditions

- A claim is being evaluated for empirical meaning, not mathematical or definitional truth.
- The claim's proponents are available to specify what evidence would change their minds.
- Observable evidence is at least conceivably accessible (the test need not be feasible immediately, only conceivable).
- The decision context cares about the claim's predictive content rather than its rhetorical persuasive power.

### Common Misapplications

- Demanding falsifiability of claims that are not empirical (mathematical theorems, value statements, definitional claims) and dismissing them when they fail the test.
- Treating any unfalsified theory as confirmed; falsifiability says only what an empirically meaningful theory must permit, not what proves it true.

### Related Models

- **Confirmation Bias** — the related error: seeking only confirming evidence; falsifiability prescribes the corrective.
- **Devil's Advocacy** — the social-process complement: structured opposition forces the falsification attempt.
- **Bayesian Reasoning** — the probabilistic complement: how to update beliefs when falsifying evidence is partial rather than decisive.

## Application Steps

1. State the claim precisely; vague claims are harder to falsify and that vagueness is itself a warning sign.
2. Ask: "What observation, if it occurred, would prove this wrong?"
3. If no such observation exists, the claim is unfalsifiable and should be treated with skepticism.
4. If a falsifying observation does exist, design a test that could produce it.
5. Run the test honestly — seek disconfirmation, not confirmation; resist the urge to add ad-hoc rescue clauses when results disconfirm.

## Detection Signals

- A claim's proponents respond to every contradicting observation with an explanation that preserves the claim.
- A theory accounts for all evidence including evidence that should logically reduce its plausibility.
- The proponents cannot answer the question "what would change your mind?"
- A debate is stalling because one side has no conditions under which they would update.
- A model, forecast, or strategic thesis is being evaluated for rigor and the falsifiability question has not been asked.

## Critical Questions

- Has the analyst stated, in advance and in writing, what observation would falsify the claim?
- If a falsifying observation has occurred, has the theory been revised, or have rescue clauses been added?
- Is the claim being evaluated empirical, or is it definitional/mathematical/normative? Wrong-category application invalidates the diagnostic.
- Is the falsifying observation actually accessible (in principle if not yet in practice)? An "in-principle but never accessible" test is weaker than one that can actually be run.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Rescue-clause accumulation** — Detection signal: each disconfirming observation generates a new auxiliary hypothesis that preserves the theory. Correction: track the rescue clauses; when their number grows large, the theory has degenerated to unfalsifiability.
- **Wrong-category application** — Detection signal: falsifiability is demanded of mathematical theorems or value statements, which are then dismissed. Correction: apply only to empirical claims.
- **Vague claim as falsifiable** — Detection signal: the proponent claims the theory is falsifiable but cannot name the falsifying observation. Correction: precision of the claim and precision of the falsification condition are coupled; vague claims fail the test even if proponents assert otherwise.
