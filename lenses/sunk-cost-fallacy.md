# Sunk Cost Fallacy

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that evaluate continuation-vs-abandonment decisions — failing projects, losing investments, dying products — when the argument for continuing is built on past expenditure rather than future expected return. The host mode supplies the project history and the forward-looking decision; the lens supplies the past-cost-irrelevance discipline and the kill-criteria design.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Past costs that cannot be recovered should not influence future decisions — only prospective costs and benefits matter. Yet people routinely throw good money (or time, or effort) after bad because abandoning an investment feels like "wasting" what came before. The fallacy turns past expenditure into a psychological trap. Rational decision-making requires asking "Given where I am now, what is the best path forward?" — never "How much have I already put in?"

### Mechanism

Loss aversion makes abandonment feel like locking in a loss; continuation preserves the hope that the prior expenditure will eventually pay off. Cognitive dissonance pressure favors decisions that justify prior decisions. Social commitment to a course of action makes reversal feel like admitting error. Together these forces produce the systematic pattern of throwing good after bad.

### Applicability Conditions

- A project, investment, or commitment has accumulated unrecoverable past costs.
- A decision is being made about future allocation.
- The future expected return is calculable independently of past costs.
- The actor has the authority to abandon.

### Common Misapplications

- Confusing sunk-cost reasoning with disregard for institutional knowledge accumulated alongside the cost.
- Treating all continuation as fallacy when continuation may have positive forward-looking value.
- Using the lens to justify abandonment of difficult projects whose forward expected value is genuinely positive.
- Counting reputational costs as if they were sunk when they affect future decisions.

### Related Models

- **Loss Aversion** — the underlying mechanism.
- **Escalation of Commitment** — group-level extension where social dynamics amplify the fallacy.
- **Pre-mortem Analysis** — what kill-criteria design draws on.

### Worked example

A startup has spent $2 million and 18 months building a product. Beta users are uninterested, the market has shifted, and a pivot is clearly needed. The founders argue that stopping "wastes" the $2 million. But that money is gone regardless — the real question is whether the next $500,000 is better spent continuing or pivoting. A disciplined analysis of future expected returns, ignoring sunk costs, shows the pivot has higher expected value.

## Application Steps

1. Separate past costs from future analysis — explicitly label what has already been spent as irrecoverable.
2. Ask: "If I were starting fresh today with no prior investment, would I choose this path?"
3. Reframe abandonment as reallocation — the resources you free up have opportunity value elsewhere.
4. Set pre-commitment kill criteria before starting projects: define conditions under which you will stop, before sunk costs accumulate emotional weight.
5. Watch for escalation of commitment in groups — social pressure ("we all agreed to this") amplifies the fallacy.

## Detection Signals

- A project is failing but the team resists cancellation because of time and money already invested.
- An argument for continuing is built primarily on past effort rather than future value.
- The analyst feels emotionally attached to a decision proportional to what it has cost.
- Team members invoke "we've come too far to stop now."
- Group cohesion depends on continued commitment to the failing course.

## Critical Questions

- Is the forward-looking analysis genuinely independent of sunk costs?
- Have institutional learnings (which are not sunk) been distinguished from sunk monetary cost?
- Are pre-committed kill criteria in place, and have they been triggered?
- Would the analyst recommend starting this project today if presented as new?
- Are reputational consequences being treated as sunk when they actually affect future actions?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Forward-vs-backward conflation** — past investment colors the forward expected-value calculation. Detection: forward EV is suspiciously high. Correction: have a fresh analyst run the EV calculation.
- **Knowledge-as-sunk error** — treating all past investment as sunk when learning accrued is forward-relevant. Detection: analyst discards genuinely useful knowledge along with sunk cost. Correction: separate knowledge (forward-relevant) from cash (sunk).
- **Kill-criteria abandonment** — pre-set criteria are re-litigated when triggered. Detection: criteria are softened to allow continuation. Correction: enforce kill decisions; adjust criteria for the next project, not the current one.
