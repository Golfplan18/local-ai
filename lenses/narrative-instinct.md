# Narrative Instinct

## Trigger

Invoked from any mode that evaluates causal explanations of complex events — post-mortems, case studies, retrospective analysis — when a clean storyline is being offered as the explanation. The host mode supplies the candidate explanation and the underlying event; the lens supplies the suspicion that the satisfying shape of the narrative is itself evidence of compression and selection.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

Humans are compulsive story-builders. We compress messy, multicausal events into clean narratives with heroes, villains, turning points, and morals. This makes the world feel comprehensible but sacrifices accuracy. Reality is driven by overlapping systems, randomness, and probabilities — not by plot arcs. The more satisfying a story feels, the more you should question whether it is true.

### Mechanism

Cognitive economy favors compressed, causally coherent representations of events because they are easier to store, recall, and communicate. Hindsight bias and the availability of a clear outcome make pre-outcome uncertainty invisible. The result: a narrative that explains the actual outcome will always feel more probable in retrospect than its true ex-ante probability warranted, and competing causal factors get pruned to keep the story clean.

### Applicability Conditions

- The event being explained is multicausal and unfolded over time.
- The explanation has a clear protagonist, turning point, or moral.
- Outcome data is available and the explanation post-dates the outcome.
- Alternative explanations are possible but absent from the narrative.

### Common Misapplications

- Using the lens to dismiss all narrative explanation, including those grounded in evidence.
- Substituting "it was random" for the work of identifying actual contributing causes.
- Applying the lens only to others' narratives while accepting one's own.

### Related Models

- **Hindsight Bias** — the underlying mechanism that makes narrative compression invisible after the fact.
- **Survivorship Bias** — the selection process that makes the narrative's evidence base unrepresentative.
- **Sensemaking** — the legitimate cousin: post-hoc story construction can be valuable when its limits are acknowledged.

### Worked example

The standard narrative of Apple's revival: Steve Jobs returned, created the iMac, then the iPod, then the iPhone — visionary genius saved the company. The narrative omits: a $150M Microsoft investment that kept Apple solvent, an industry-wide shift to digital music that created the iPod's market, carrier subsidies that made iPhone pricing viable, and thousands of engineering decisions by people whose names never appear in the story. The neat arc feels true but compresses a complex system into a hero's journey.

## Application Steps

1. Notice the narrative — identify the protagonist, the turning point, and the moral.
2. Count how many causal factors have been collapsed into this story.
3. List alternative causes, contributing randomness, and factors the narrative omits.
4. Check whether the same narrative logic would have predicted the outcome in advance.
5. Replace the story with a probabilistic explanation — what range of outcomes was plausible?

## Detection Signals

- A post-mortem or case study tells a clean causal story about a complex outcome.
- Someone attributes a success or failure to a single decision or person.
- Hindsight is making a past event feel inevitable ("it was obvious in retrospect").
- An argument relies on compelling anecdote rather than systematic evidence.
- An explanation fits suspiciously well — every detail fits the storyline with no residue.

## Critical Questions

- Is the narrative falsifiable in advance, or only after the outcome is known?
- Are there contributing causes that the narrative cannot accommodate without weakening?
- Would the same explanatory logic, applied prospectively, have predicted this outcome among the field of plausible outcomes?
- Whose perspective is centered in the narrative, and whose is omitted?
- Does the story require coincidences or unstated assumptions to hold together?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Counter-narrative trap** — replacing one clean story with another clean story rather than embracing multicausal complexity. Detection: the new explanation has a different protagonist but the same single-cause shape. Correction: produce a list of causes weighted by contribution, not a replacement narrative.
- **Cynicism collapse** — using the lens to dismiss all causal explanation as "just a story." Detection: analyst refuses to commit to any explanation. Correction: name the causes you have evidence for, with appropriate uncertainty bands.
- **Asymmetric application** — applying the lens to others' narratives but accepting one's own. Detection: skepticism is reserved for explanations that conflict with the analyst's prior. Correction: run the lens on your preferred explanation first.
