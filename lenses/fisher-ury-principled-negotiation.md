# Fisher Ury Principled Negotiation

## Trigger

Invoked from within `interest-mapping` (T13), `principled-negotiation` (T13), and `third-side` (T13) when those modes need the kernel framework for transforming a positional bargaining situation into an interests-based negotiation. The host mode supplies the situation: a dispute, a deal, or a multi-party conflict in which parties have stated incompatible positions. The lens supplies the four principles that structure the negotiation — separating people from problem, focusing on interests rather than positions, inventing options for mutual gain, and insisting on objective criteria — together with BATNA as the source of negotiating power. The framework is *prescriptive*: it tells the analyst how to restructure the negotiation, not merely how to describe it.

## Core Structure

Four principles plus BATNA. Each component has a definition (what it is), distinguishing features (what it is not), and a worked-example sketch.

### Component 1 — Separate the People from the Problem

**Definition.** Treat the substantive dispute (the problem) as a different object from the relationship between the parties. Negotiate the problem hard while preserving the working relationship; do not let positional conflict become personal antagonism, and do not let the desire to preserve the relationship soften the substantive analysis.

**Distinguishing features.** This is not "be nice" (which softens substance) and not "be tough" (which damages relationship). It is the discipline of running two tracks: the substantive track addresses the problem, the relational track addresses the working relationship. The two tracks are decoupled — a hard substantive position can be paired with a soft relational stance ("hard on the problem, soft on the people").

**Worked-example sketch.** Two co-founders disputing equity split. Positional version: each treats the other's number as a personal slight, the relationship corrodes, the dispute escalates into questions of trust and worth. Principled version: the substantive question (what equity split fits the contributions and risks?) is separated from the relational question (do we still trust each other to build this?). The relational track is repaired through acknowledgment and active listening; the substantive track is then negotiated on its merits without the relational charge.

### Component 2 — Focus on Interests, Not Positions

**Definition.** A *position* is what a party says it wants ("I want the corner office"). An *interest* is the underlying need or concern that produces the position ("I want quiet to focus, recognition for my role, sunlight in the morning"). Positions are typically singular and incompatible; interests are typically multiple and have substantial overlap with the other party's interests, opening room for trades.

**Distinguishing features.** Identifying interests is not asking "why do you want X?" once; it is asking until the answer is in terms of needs, fears, or values that could be satisfied multiple ways. The test for an interest is whether removing the position would leave the interest intact and seeking other satisfaction. Most positional deadlocks dissolve when the interests behind the positions are surfaced and turn out to be compatible.

**Worked-example sketch.** A buyer and seller deadlocked over price ($95K vs. $110K). Position-mode: the dispute is binary, with no path to agreement. Interest-mode: the seller needs quick close (cash flow); the buyer needs warranty coverage (risk reduction). The agreement: $100K with seller financing the warranty extension, satisfying both interests at a price between the positions.

### Component 3 — Invent Options for Mutual Gain

**Definition.** Before deciding, generate a wide set of possible agreements that meet both parties' interests. Defer judgment during option-generation; the goal is volume and variety, not initial quality. Look explicitly for options that *enlarge the pie* (create joint value) before negotiating how to divide it.

**Distinguishing features.** This is not brainstorming-as-formality; it is the active discipline of looking for shared and complementary interests, differing forecasts, differing risk preferences, and differing time horizons that can be turned into trades. The four obstacles Fisher and Ury name are: premature judgment, single-answer search, fixed-pie assumption, and "their problem is theirs to solve." All four collapse the option space.

**Worked-example sketch.** A landlord and tenant disputing lease renewal. Single-answer search: the negotiation oscillates between two rent figures. Option-invention: a third option emerges (longer lease term in exchange for capped annual increases), satisfying the landlord's interest in occupancy stability and the tenant's interest in rent predictability — neither party would have constructed this option had they remained in single-answer mode.

### Component 4 — Insist on Objective Criteria

**Definition.** Frame the negotiation as a joint search for the standard that should govern the outcome — market price, expert opinion, precedent, scientific finding, legal standard, equal treatment, reciprocity — rather than a contest of wills. Once a criterion is agreed, applying it produces an agreement that both parties can defend and that neither was forced to concede.

**Distinguishing features.** This is not capitulation to the other side's preferred standard; it is insisting that *some* objective standard be agreed and then jointly identifying what that standard is. The criterion's authority comes from being legitimate (defensible to a third party) and applicable to both sides. When parties cannot agree on a single criterion, the framework prescribes agreeing on a *procedure* for selecting one (e.g., "let's commission an independent appraisal").

**Worked-example sketch.** A divorcing couple disputing the value of a jointly owned business. Will-contest version: each side asserts the value that benefits them; agreement is impossible. Objective-criteria version: agree on a standard (the average of three independent business valuations) and a procedure for selecting valuators; the resulting number is binding because the standard is, not because either party conceded.

### Source of Power — BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)

**Definition.** A party's BATNA is the best outcome that party can secure without reaching agreement with the other side. The strength of a party's negotiating position is determined not by what it demands but by how good its BATNA is — that is, how little it loses by walking away. The reservation point (the worst agreement the party should accept) is set by the BATNA: do not accept any agreement worse than your BATNA.

**Distinguishing features.** BATNA is what one *actually does* if no agreement is reached, not what one threatens to do. It must be developed concretely (what specific alternative deal, what specific course of action) and tested for realism. A weak BATNA cannot be improved by bluffing; it must be improved by actually building alternatives. The framework's prescriptive use: invest as much energy in improving the BATNA as in negotiating the deal, because the BATNA is what determines the negotiating power.

**Worked-example sketch.** A job candidate negotiating salary. Without an alternative offer (weak BATNA), the candidate's leverage is rhetorical only. With a competing offer at $X (strong BATNA), the candidate can credibly hold to a number above $X; the employer's choice is to match or lose the candidate. The candidate's job before the negotiation is to develop the alternative offer, not to rehearse arguments about why they deserve a higher salary.

### Caveat (Voss critique; Debate D6)

Principled negotiation assumes a counterparty who is, at minimum, *willing* to negotiate on the merits. When the counterparty is adversarial, manipulative, or operating in bad faith — using positional bargaining as a power-move, dismissing your interests, refusing to recognize objective criteria — the framework's prescriptions can leave the principled negotiator exposed. Voss's *Never Split the Difference* (2016) argues that high-stakes negotiation often requires tactical empathy, calibrated questions, and accusation audits that go beyond the Fisher-Ury repertoire. This is logged in Debate D6 (Wave-2 debates): use Fisher-Ury for cooperative or merely-stuck counterparties; supplement with Voss-style tools when the counterparty is adversarial. The two are not strictly incompatible — Voss's tactical empathy is a sophisticated implementation of "separate people from problem" — but the Fisher-Ury default assumption (rational counterparty) does not always hold and the analyst should detect when it doesn't.

## Application Steps

1. Receive the negotiation situation from the host mode (parties, stated positions, context, stakes).
2. Apply **Separate People from Problem**: identify the substantive vs. relational components; track them separately throughout.
3. Apply **Interests, Not Positions**: for each party, surface the underlying interests behind the stated positions; identify shared, complementary, and conflicting interests.
4. Apply **Invent Options for Mutual Gain**: generate a wide option set defer-judgment style; look for trades across differing interests and differing forecasts/preferences.
5. Apply **Objective Criteria**: identify candidate standards both parties could accept; if none agreed, agree on a procedure for selecting one.
6. Develop **BATNA** for each party: identify the realistic alternative each has if no agreement is reached; set reservation points accordingly.
7. Run the **adversarial-counterparty check** (Voss caveat): is the counterparty negotiating on merits, or running a positional/manipulative game? If the latter, flag for supplementary tools.
8. Return the structured analysis to the host mode: framework application + BATNA assessment + adversarial check.

## Detection Signals

- The host mode is dealing with a multi-party situation in which parties have declared incompatible positions.
- The dispute has been characterized by the parties as binary (one wins, one loses) but the underlying needs may be multidimensional.
- The negotiation has stalled in positional bargaining (each side restating its position with greater volume).
- The parties have ongoing relationships at stake and the dispute risks corroding them.
- The host mode flags that the analyst needs to map interests beneath stated positions before proceeding.
- A third party is being asked to mediate or facilitate the dispute and needs the framework to structure the intervention.

## Critical Questions

- Are the *interests* identified, or has the analyst stopped at the level of stated positions (or restated them in slightly different language)?
- Is the people-problem separation actually maintained, or is the substantive analysis being softened to preserve relationship (or relationship being damaged to win on substance)?
- Has the option set been generated with judgment deferred, or has premature evaluation pre-collapsed it?
- Is the proposed objective criterion *legitimate* — defensible to a third party as principled — or merely the preferred standard of one side?
- Have the BATNAs been developed concretely (with specific alternatives), or are they rhetorical ("I have other options")?
- Has the adversarial-counterparty check been run? If the counterparty is operating in bad faith, the framework's prescriptions need supplementation, not straight application.
- Are power asymmetries acknowledged? Principled negotiation does not erase power differences; it tells the weaker party how to use what leverage they have, but cannot manufacture leverage that does not exist.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Position-laundering** — interests are nominally surfaced but turn out to be the original positions in slightly different language ("my interest is to get $100K" rather than "my interest is to secure adequate cash for X"). Detection: the "interests" identified would be satisfied only by the originally-stated position. Correction: keep asking "why" until the answer is in terms of underlying needs.
- **Niceness-substitution** — the principle "separate people from problem" gets read as "be nice" and the substantive analysis is softened to preserve relationship. Detection: the analyst's recommended posture concedes substance for relational warmth. Correction: explicitly check that the substantive position has not been softened; the discipline is "hard on the problem, soft on the people."
- **Symmetric-rationality assumption** — the framework is applied to a counterparty who is operating in bad faith, treating principled negotiation as naïve and exploiting the negotiator's restraint. Detection: the counterparty refuses to surface interests, dismisses objective criteria, and uses positional pressure tactics. Correction: switch to or supplement with Voss-style tactical empathy and accusation audit; the cooperative default does not apply.
- **BATNA inflation** — the BATNA is rhetorically asserted ("I can walk") rather than concretely developed. Detection: the analyst cannot describe what specifically would happen if the negotiation failed. Correction: develop the BATNA as a specific alternative with realistic terms; if the actual BATNA is weak, name that and adjust the negotiating posture accordingly.
- **Pseudo-objective criterion** — the "objective" criterion is one party's preferred standard dressed up in neutral language. Detection: the proposed criterion would consistently favor one side under realistic application. Correction: legitimacy test — would a disinterested third party regard this criterion as principled? If not, generate alternatives.
- **Pie-not-enlarged failure** — option-invention is performed but produces only redistributions of the existing pie, not new value. Detection: every proposed option is a different way to split the same total. Correction: explicitly look for differing forecasts, differing risk preferences, differing time horizons, and complementary interests as sources of joint value.
