# Public Choice Theory

## Trigger

Invoked when a mode analyzes political, bureaucratic, regulatory, or institutional behavior and needs to treat public actors as incentive-responsive agents rather than as pure representatives of public interest. The host mode supplies the policy or stakeholder situation; the lens supplies the incentive scan.

## Core Structure

Public choice applies economic reasoning to political and institutional decision-making. It asks what elected officials, agencies, voters, firms, interest groups, and bureaucrats are rewarded for doing.

1. **Concentrated benefits, diffuse costs.** Small groups may organize strongly for benefits spread across a large public.
2. **Rent seeking.** Actors spend resources to capture policy advantage rather than create new value.
3. **Bureaucratic self-interest.** Agencies may seek budget, scope, autonomy, and survival.
4. **Rational ignorance.** Voters or citizens may not invest in detailed knowledge when individual influence is tiny.
5. **Collective-action asymmetry.** Groups with lower coordination costs dominate groups with broader but weaker shared interests.

## Application Steps

1. Identify the institutional actors and their formal goals.
2. Map the private or organizational incentives each actor faces.
3. Identify who receives concentrated benefits and who bears diffuse costs.
4. Look for rent-seeking, regulatory capture, budget maximization, or symbolic incentives.
5. Separate public-interest justification from incentive-compatible explanation.
6. Return both the charitable policy rationale and the public-choice incentive account.

## Detection Signals

- A policy benefits a narrow organized group while costs are spread widely.
- A regulator appears aligned with the regulated industry.
- An agency's recommended solution expands its budget, authority, or permanence.
- Stakeholders behave differently from their stated public-purpose rationale.
- The question "who benefits?" requires institutional incentive structure.

## Critical Questions

- What does each actor gain by supporting or resisting the policy?
- Who pays, and are the payers organized enough to notice?
- Which actor has the lowest coordination cost?
- What bureaucratic survival or expansion incentive is present?
- Is the public-interest story compatible with the incentive story?
- What evidence would distinguish sincere public purpose from rent seeking?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Cynical reduction** - Detection: every public action is assumed corrupt. Correction: compare incentive explanation with good-faith policy rationale.
- **Actor flattening** - Detection: "the government" is treated as one mind. Correction: separate voters, elected officials, agencies, staff, courts, firms, and interest groups.
- **Benefit-only scan** - Detection: beneficiaries are mapped but cost diffusion is ignored. Correction: identify who pays and why they may not mobilize.
- **Capture overclaim** - Detection: capture is asserted without mechanism. Correction: name the channel: revolving door, information dependence, lobbying, budget, or legal constraint.
