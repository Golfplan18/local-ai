# System 1 / System 2

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that diagnose judgment errors, design decision processes, or analyze cognitive load — when an answer feels obvious and the question is whether System 1 has substituted an easier question for the actual one. The host mode supplies the judgment and its context; the lens supplies the dual-process diagnostic that distinguishes effortless intuition from deliberate analysis.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

The mind operates in two modes. System 1 is fast, automatic, and effortless — it recognizes patterns, generates intuitions, and delivers instant judgments. System 2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful — it handles logic, computation, and careful analysis. Most of the time System 1 runs the show, and System 2 lazily endorses its conclusions. Errors arise when System 1 faces problems that require System 2 but the switch never happens. Daniel Kahneman: "A reliable way to make people believe in falsehoods is frequent repetition, because familiarity is not easily distinguished from truth."

### Mechanism

System 1 operates through associative memory, pattern matching, and learned heuristics. It produces fluent answers quickly and registers the answers as feeling correct. System 2 must be effortfully engaged; without engagement, System 1's output is accepted by default. System 2 is also depleted by cognitive load, fatigue, and distraction, making System 1 more dominant exactly when stakes may demand the opposite. Question substitution is a key System 1 move: an easier related question is answered while the actor believes they answered the original.

### Applicability Conditions

- A judgment is being made, especially under uncertainty or time pressure.
- The judgment has access to both intuition and analysis.
- The actor is operating with normal human cognition.
- Stakes are high enough to warrant evaluating which system should be in charge.

### Common Misapplications

- Treating System 1 as the enemy when it is efficient and often right.
- Assuming System 2 engagement is always feasible regardless of cognitive load.
- Ignoring expertise: in domains of true expertise, System 1 can outperform System 2.
- Conflating dual-process theory with neuroanatomical claims it does not make.

### Related Models

- **Cognitive Bias Catalog** — the System 1 errors that have been catalogued.
- **Heuristics** — the operational form of System 1's pattern matching.
- **Question Substitution** — the central System 1 move that produces error.

### Worked example

A hiring manager interviews a candidate who is articulate, confident, and shares the manager's alma mater. System 1 instantly produces a positive impression — "strong candidate." System 2 never engages because the feeling of certainty is already there. The manager skips the structured scoring rubric. Later, the hire underperforms on exactly the competencies the rubric was designed to test. The structured interview process was a System 2 forcing function; bypassing it let System 1's halo effect drive the decision unchecked.

## Application Steps

1. Notice the feeling of fluency — when an answer feels effortless, flag it for review.
2. Ask whether the question being answered is the question that was asked (System 1 substitutes easier questions).
3. Slow down: write out the reasoning, do the math, check the logic explicitly.
4. Use forcing functions — checklists, structured frameworks, required wait periods — to engage System 2 where it matters.
5. Recognize that System 1 is not the enemy; it is efficient and often right. The goal is knowing when to override it, not eliminating it.

## Detection Signals

- An answer comes to mind instantly and feels certain — especially if the question is complex.
- A decision is being made under time pressure, cognitive load, or emotional arousal.
- People are anchoring on the first number, frame, or narrative they encountered.
- A problem has a seductive but wrong intuitive answer (like the bat-and-ball problem).
- Fatigue or distraction is reducing the capacity for deliberate thought.

## Critical Questions

- Is the actor's intuition coming from genuine domain expertise or from surface pattern-matching?
- Has the actor checked whether the answered question is the asked question?
- Are forcing functions in place for high-stakes judgments?
- Is cognitive load high enough that System 2 cannot reliably engage even when needed?
- Is the analyst over-correcting, demanding System 2 for routine judgments where System 1 suffices?

## Common Failure Modes

- **System-1-as-default endorsement** — accepting fluent answers without evaluation. Detection: judgments are made without explicit reasoning. Correction: build pause-and-check habits for high-stakes decisions.
- **Question substitution** — answering an easier related question. Detection: the answer is fluent but doesn't actually address the question asked. Correction: explicitly state the question, then state what is being answered, and check for match.
- **System 2 over-correction** — demanding effortful analysis for routine judgments. Detection: decision speed degrades without proportional accuracy gain. Correction: reserve System 2 engagement for high-stakes or novel decisions.
