# Procedural Justice

## Trigger

Invoked from modes that design decision processes where affected parties must accept outcomes they did not choose — governance design, layoffs, policy rollouts, dispute resolution — when outcome quality alone will not produce compliance or commitment. The host mode supplies the decision context and the affected parties; the lens supplies the four-pillar process design (voice, transparency, consistency, respect) that generates legitimacy.

## Core Structure

### Core Insight

People evaluate fairness not only by outcomes but by the process used to reach them. A fair process — one that includes voice, transparency, consistency, and respectful treatment — generates commitment and compliance even when the outcome goes against an individual's preference. Tom Tyler's research showed that people who lose in court are more likely to comply with the ruling if they felt the trial was procedurally fair. The process is not just a means to a decision; it is itself a source of legitimacy.

### Mechanism

When affected parties experience voice (genuine opportunity to be heard), transparency (visible reasoning), consistency (similar cases treated similarly), and respect (dignified treatment), they integrate the decision into their identity as legitimate even when it disadvantages them. The procedural experience updates beliefs about the system's fairness independent of the outcome. Compliance then becomes intrinsic rather than dependent on enforcement.

### Applicability Conditions

- A decision will inevitably disappoint some stakeholders.
- The decision-maker has discretion over process design.
- Affected parties' future cooperation, compliance, or morale matters.
- Time and resources permit a process more elaborate than the minimum.

### Common Misapplications

- Theatrical voice — soliciting input that the decision-maker will not consider.
- Transparency as data dump rather than reasoning disclosure.
- Consistency that ignores material differences between cases.
- Respect performed superficially while substantive treatment is dismissive.

### Related Models

- **Distributive Justice** — fairness of outcomes; complementary axis to procedural fairness.
- **Stakeholder Analysis** — identifying whose procedural experience matters.
- **Psychological Safety** — the team-level analog: voice without fear of retribution.

### Worked example

A company must lay off 15% of staff. Approach A: leadership decides behind closed doors, announces the cuts, and offers severance. Approach B: leadership explains the financial situation transparently, asks managers to identify roles that could be consolidated, gives affected employees two weeks to apply for remaining positions, and provides clear criteria. Both produce the same layoffs. But in Approach B, remaining employees report higher trust in leadership, lower voluntary turnover, and stronger engagement.

## Application Steps

1. Ensure voice: give affected parties a genuine opportunity to present their perspective before the decision is made.
2. Ensure transparency: explain the criteria, the process, and the reasoning behind the final decision.
3. Ensure consistency: apply the same process and criteria to similar cases — perceived inconsistency destroys legitimacy.
4. Ensure respect: treat people with dignity throughout, especially when delivering unfavorable outcomes.
5. Close the loop: after the decision, explain how input was considered — even if the outcome didn't change, acknowledgment matters.

## Detection Signals

- A decision will inevitably disappoint some stakeholders and you need their continued cooperation.
- Trust or morale is low and outcomes alone won't rebuild it.
- Designing governance, review, or dispute resolution processes.
- A technically correct decision was rejected — the process may have felt unfair.
- Leading change in organizations where affected people had no say in the direction.

## Critical Questions

- Is the voice opportunity genuine, or is the decision already made?
- Is the consistency real (similar cases treated similarly) or apparent (cases forced into a single template that ignores differences)?
- Is transparency about the reasoning, or only about the outcome?
- Does the process treat people as participants or as objects of decisions made about them?
- Has the decision-maker explained how input was considered, even when it didn't change the outcome?

## Common Failure Modes

- **Voice theater** — collecting input without using it. Detection: input is solicited but the decision is unchanged. Correction: either incorporate input genuinely or be honest that the decision is fixed and the conversation is consultation, not input.
- **Inconsistency rationalization** — treating cases differently and post-hoc justifying with case-specific factors. Detection: similar cases produce different outcomes without principled explanation. Correction: write the criteria in advance and apply them publicly.
- **Respect-as-tone** — polite delivery of disrespectful substance. Detection: people report feeling disrespected despite no rude language. Correction: examine substantive treatment, not just communication style.
